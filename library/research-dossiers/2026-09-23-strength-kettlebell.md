## 1. Executive summary — read this before the section detail

- **Kettlebell-for-swimmers-specifically: still no citable source.** This
  confirms rather than reopens `07`'s existing, correct conclusion. Every
  kettlebell EMG/biomechanics study found below is in general lifters,
  athletes from other sports, or clinical/laboratory populations — never
  swimmers, never an endurance-transfer design. Kettlebell exercise
  selection stays `Coach judgment:`, informed by general
  biomechanics/EMG evidence about what a movement *does*, not evidence that
  it helps *this athlete's* sport.
- **The general kettlebell EMG literature is real, small, and useful only
  for movement-pattern mapping, not dosing.** Studies are typically
  n=10-16, single-session, healthy young adults (often male, often
  non-endurance-athlete). They can defensibly answer "does the KB swing
  load the posterior chain / does the bottoms-up press change scapular
  muscle recruitment" — useful for `07`'s exercise-selection layer — but
  say nothing about chronic training outcomes, injury risk, or dosing.
- **The single most important overhead-KB-safety finding reframes the
  question `07` needs to ask.** Scapular dyskinesis — the classical reason
  overhead work gets restricted in a shoulder-injury-prone overhead
  athlete — is **not, by itself, a validated predictor of shoulder injury**
  per a 2021 systematic review/meta-analysis (Hogan et al.). This does not
  mean overhead KB work is automatically safe for this athlete post-injury
  — her injury is a real, individual event needing individualized
  clearance, per `21-shoulder-health-and-load.md`'s return-to-sport
  criteria — but it means "her scapula looks a bit dyskinetic" is not by
  itself a reason to ban overhead kettlebell work forever. Frame it as
  *load and criteria matter more than the abstract idea of "overhead is
  dangerous."*
- **Masters/postmenopausal strength evidence is strong and directly
  relevant — this is the dossier's best new grounding for `07`.** LIFTMOR
  (Watson et al. 2018) is a genuine, well-verified RCT showing
  twice-weekly, ~30-min, *heavy* (not light) resistance + impact training
  is safe and improves BMD/function in postmenopausal women with low bone
  mass — directly counters an intuition that a postmenopausal athlete
  should lift light. A 2025/2026 sarcopenia meta-analysis in older women
  corroborates strength/function gains from resistance training generally,
  with the honest caveat that muscle-*mass* gains are less consistent than
  strength/function gains.
- **Concurrent-training dosing literature has matured past a blanket
  "interference effect" story, and this matters for scheduling around 3-5x
  pool sessions/week.** The classic Wilson et al. (2012) meta-analysis
  found real (small-moderate) interference; more recent work (Schumann et
  al. 2022; a 2023/2024 sex-and-training-status meta-analysis) finds
  hypertrophy/strength are largely preserved under concurrent training when
  volume/frequency are reasonable, with the residual interference
  concentrated in *explosive*/power strength and worsened by same-session
  ordering — actionable for how `07`/`/adapt` sequence strength relative to
  pool sessions, beyond what `07` currently says.
- **Heavy-load endurance-strength dosing is well-documented in a specific,
  reproducible protocol shape** (low-rep, high-%1RM, low-volume, 2x/week,
  block-periodized) from the Rønnestad/Vikmoen research line, including
  a study specifically **in well-trained female athletes** — a closer
  population match than the general Rønnestad & Mujika (2014) review `07`
  already cites. This is about general strength-for-endurance-performance
  dosing, not injury-prevention dosing, so it complements rather than
  replaces `07`'s existing swimmer-specific rotator-cuff dosing section.
- **KB-specific progression with limited bell increments (the classic 4 kg
  jump problem): no research exists on this at all.** This is an equipment
  logistics question, not a physiology question, and stays 100%
  `Coach judgment:` — reps, tempo, unilateral loading, and density
  (more work in the same time) are the standard non-load progression
  levers used in general S&C practice, not something a citation can ground.

## 2. Verified sources by topic

### Topic A — Kettlebell movement-pattern / EMG evidence (general population, exercise-selection use only)

- **✓ Lyons B.C., Mayo J.J., Tucker W.S., Wax B., Hendrix R.C. (2017)** —
  "Electromyographical Comparison of Muscle Activation Patterns Across
  Three Commonly Performed Kettlebell Exercises" — *Journal of Strength and
  Conditioning Research*, 31(9):2363-2370. **Verification:** title, full
  author list, journal, year, volume/issue/pages confirmed via PubMed, the
  journal's own site (Ovid/LWW), and ResearchGate.
  **Finding:** 14 resistance-trained subjects performed the one-arm swing,
  one-arm swing-style snatch, and one-arm clean; EMG recorded from biceps
  brachii, anterior/posterior deltoid, erector spinae, vastus lateralis,
  biceps femoris, external oblique, gluteus maximus. All three movements
  drove substantial posterior-chain and trunk activation; the three
  exercises differed in *which* muscles peaked and when, supporting
  exercise variety rather than one "best" KB ballistic movement.
  **Population/use limit:** general resistance-trained adults, single
  session, no swimmers, no chronic outcome — informs exercise *selection*
  logic only.
  **Tag if used in `07`:** `Coach judgment:` (general S&C evidence used to
  justify a movement-pattern choice, not `[EVIDENCE: swim]` or
  `[ADAPTED]` — the population isn't an adjacent endurance discipline, it's
  a general-lifter biomechanics study).

- **✓ Zebis M.K., Skotte J., Andersen C.H., et al. (2013)** — "Kettlebell
  swing targets semitendinosus and supine leg curl targets biceps femoris:
  an EMG study with rehabilitation implications" — *British Journal of
  Sports Medicine*, 47(18):1192-1198. **Verification:** title, authors,
  journal, year, volume/issue/pages confirmed via PubMed and multiple
  independent citation records.
  **Finding:** 16 female elite handball/soccer players; KB swing and
  Romanian deadlift drove very high (73-115% MVC) medial-hamstring
  (semitendinosus) activation, specifically higher than lateral hamstring
  (biceps femoris) — relevant to hip-hinge/posterior-chain exercise
  selection and (per the paper's own framing) knee-injury-prevention
  programming, not shoulder or swim-specific.
  **Why it matters for this dossier:** strong, specific evidence that the
  KB swing is a genuine posterior-chain/hip-hinge loading tool — supports
  `07`'s own logical mapping of "swing/deadlift -> posterior chain" in
  Question 1, even though the population is field-sport athletes, not
  swimmers. **Tag:** `Coach judgment:` (same reasoning as Lyons above).

- **✓ St-Onge E., Robb A., Beach T.A.C., Howarth S.J. (2018/2019)** — "A
  descriptive analysis of shoulder muscle activities during individual
  stages of the Turkish Get-Up exercise" — *Journal of Bodywork and
  Movement Therapies*. **Verification:** title and full author list
  confirmed via the publisher (ScienceDirect) listing; exact print
  year/volume/issue not independently pinned down this pass (the article
  appears to be available online in 2018 with print volume/issue not
  confirmed) — **flag this bibliographic detail for the build agent to
  confirm before citing a specific year in `reference_list.md`.**
  **Finding:** bilateral surface EMG from 8 glenohumeral muscle groups
  (including rotator cuff: teres minor, subscapularis, supraspinatus,
  infraspinatus) across the Turkish get-up's 7 stages. Greatest muscular
  demand occurred during the press-to-elbow-support and leg-sweep stages;
  the exercise drives genuine rotator-cuff and scapular-stabilizer
  activity, not just a "core/stability" label.
  **Why it matters for Question 1:** the strongest available evidence that
  the Turkish get-up specifically loads rotator-cuff muscle groups (not
  just anecdote) — directly relevant to whether it belongs in a
  shoulder-injury-affected athlete's program, though the study doesn't
  address *safety* for an injured shoulder, only that the movement is
  genuinely rotator-cuff-demanding (which cuts both ways: real training
  stimulus, and real load on a joint that has been injured).
  **Tag:** `Coach judgment:` — real EMG evidence for what the exercise
  *does*, not evidence it is *appropriate* for this athlete's injury
  history; the appropriateness question routes through
  `21-shoulder-health-and-load.md`'s return-to-sport criteria instead.

- **✓ Ellestad S.H., Holcomb T.P., Swiergol A.M., Holmstrup M.E., Dicus J.R.
  (2024)** — "The Quantification of Muscle Activation During the Loaded
  Carry Movement Pattern" — *International Journal of Exercise Science*,
  17(1):480-490. **Verification:** title, full author list, journal, year,
  volume/issue/pages confirmed via PubMed, the journal (Digital Commons/
  WKU), and PMC.
  **Finding:** compared farmer's carry/hold and suitcase carry/hold.
  Walking with a load produced significantly higher bilateral trunk
  activation (longissimus, multifidus, rectus abdominis, external oblique)
  than simply holding the same load stationary; the suitcase (one-hand)
  carry drove greater ipsilateral rectus abdominis/external oblique
  activation than the suitcase hold — i.e., **the walking/gait component,
  not just the isometric hold, is what drives the anti-lateral-flexion/
  anti-rotation trunk demand.**
  **Why it matters for Question 1:** directly supports the "carries" answer
  to the anti-rotation/core question — this is the closest thing in this
  dossier to a clean mechanistic answer for why farmer/suitcase/rack
  carries are a defensible anti-rotation core choice, not just tradition.
  **Tag:** `Coach judgment:` (general population, mechanistic EMG evidence,
  not swim- or endurance-specific).

- **~ "Electromyographic analysis of shoulder-complex muscles performing
  overhead presses with dumbbell, kettlebell, and bottom-up kettlebell"**
  — *Journal of Bodywork and Movement Therapies*, 2023/2024 (PubMed record
  exists; publisher listing confirms title/journal/scope). **Full author
  byline not retrieved this pass** despite repeated search — marked `~`
  rather than `✓` for that reason; do not cite by author name until the
  build agent confirms the byline directly from the journal or PubMed.
  **Finding (from confirmed abstract-level content):** 28 male Division
  III collegiate baseball players performed matched-weight overhead presses
  with a dumbbell, a traditionally-gripped kettlebell, and a bottoms-up-
  gripped kettlebell; the bottoms-up kettlebell grip produced **greater
  serratus anterior activation** than both the traditional-grip kettlebell
  and the dumbbell.
  **Why it's relevant despite the confirmation gap:** it's the most direct
  available evidence for "does a bottoms-up kettlebell press change
  scapular-stabilizer recruitment" — the mechanistic hook for Question 1's
  "is overhead KB work advisable, and does bottoms-up change the risk
  profile" question. **Population limit is real and important:** male
  collegiate baseball players (an overhead-throwing population, not
  swimmers, not masters, not female) — even once the byline is confirmed,
  this would stay `Coach judgment:`, not an evidence tag, given the
  population mismatch stacks on top of the general-kettlebell-evidence
  caveat already established.

- **~ "Exercise-specific shoulder muscle activation across kettlebell
  variations: A pilot EMG study"** — *Journal of Electromyography and
  Kinesiology*, September 2025 (publisher listing confirms title/journal/
  date/scope). **Full author byline not retrieved this pass** — marked `~`
  for the same reason as above; confirm authors before citing.
  **Finding:** 10 healthy participants, 4 kettlebell exercises (press,
  bottoms-up press, two-arm swing, single-arm swing); measured upper
  trapezius, serratus anterior, infraspinatus. The KB **swing** produced
  the highest serratus anterior activity (scapular stability); the
  **bottoms-up press** produced elevated upper-trapezius activation but
  *under*-recruited infraspinatus relative to other variations — i.e., a
  **less favorable** posterior-rotator-cuff recruitment pattern than its
  reputation suggests.
  **Why it matters and why it's a genuine caution, not just a data point:**
  this is the one source in this dossier that pushes back on the popular
  assumption that "bottoms-up = automatically shoulder-friendly." Pilot
  study (n=10) — treat as a caution to weigh, not a settled finding, but
  it is the honest counter-evidence to the more commonly repeated
  bottoms-up-press-is-great narrative, and should be represented that way
  in `07` rather than only citing the positive finding above. **Tag:**
  `Coach judgment:` pending author confirmation; even confirmed, this
  would remain general-population mechanistic evidence, not swim- or
  injury-population evidence.

### Topic B — Overhead work and shoulder injury risk: reframing "is overhead advisable"

- **✓ Hogan C., Corbett J.-A., Ashton S., Perraton L., Frame R., Dakic J.
  (2021)** — "Scapular Dyskinesis Is Not an Isolated Risk Factor for
  Shoulder Injury in Athletes: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis" —
  *American Journal of Sports Medicine*, 49(10):2843-2853. **Verification:**
  title, full author list, journal, year, volume/issue/pages confirmed via
  PubMed, the journal (SAGE), and Monash University's own publication
  record.
  **Finding:** across the pooled prospective athlete cohorts, scapular
  dyskinesis alone did not predict future shoulder injury (in one cited
  cohort of 923 athletes, 46% showed dyskinesis; 25% of those developed
  injury vs. 21% of those without dyskinesis — not a meaningfully different
  rate).
  **Why this matters for the overhead-KB-advisability question:** the
  intuitive chain of reasoning — "overhead work can look/feel like it
  worsens scapular mechanics -> scapular dyskinesis predicts injury ->
  therefore avoid overhead work" — has a broken middle link. This does
  **not** mean overhead kettlebell work is cleared for this specific
  athlete post-injury (that's an individualized return-to-sport decision
  per `21-shoulder-health-and-load.md`, not a population-level statistic),
  but it means the *general* case against overhead training for an
  overhead athlete needs a better mechanism than "her scapula moves
  differently than ideal" — pain, strength asymmetry, and load
  tolerance (the actual `21` return-to-sport criteria) are the load-bearing
  variables, not scapular kinematics in isolation.
  **Tag:** `[ADAPTED: general-endurance]` is a poor fit (this is athletic-
  population injury-risk epidemiology, not an endurance-training
  adaptation) — better tagged **`Coach judgment:` informed by a verified
  systematic review**, analogous to how `21-shoulder-health-and-load.md`
  already handles the scapular-dyskinesis-adjacent literature it cites.
  Confidence: medium (systematic review/meta-analysis; but injury
  epidemiology in mixed athlete cohorts, not a randomized intervention, and
  not swim-specific).

### Topic C — Masters / postmenopausal strength evidence

- **✓ Watson S.L., Weeks B.K., Weis L.J., Harding A.T., Horan S.A., Beck
  B.R. (2018)** — "High-Intensity Resistance and Impact Training Improves
  Bone Mineral Density and Physical Function in Postmenopausal Women With
  Osteopenia and Osteoporosis: The LIFTMOR Randomized Controlled Trial" —
  *Journal of Bone and Mineral Research*, 33(2):211-220. **Verification:**
  full author list, journal, year, volume/issue/pages confirmed via the
  publisher (Wiley/Oxford Academic), PubMed, and ResearchGate.
  **Protocol:** postmenopausal women with low-to-very-low bone mass
  randomized to 8 months of **twice-weekly, ~30-minute, supervised**
  high-intensity progressive resistance training (deadlift, overhead press,
  back squat pattern per the LIFTMOR program design) plus impact loading,
  vs. a low-intensity home program.
  **Findings:** the high-intensity program was **safe** (no vertebral
  fractures) and improved bone mineral density and physical function more
  than the low-intensity comparator — directly countering the common
  assumption that osteoporotic/osteopenic postmenopausal women should
  avoid heavy lifting.
  **Why this is the single best new citation in this dossier:** it is
  exactly this athlete's population (postmenopausal, masters), it directly
  answers the "is heavy lifting safe/appropriate" question `07` doesn't yet
  address, and it's a genuine RCT, not an adaptation.
  **Tag:** `[EVIDENCE: general-endurance]` is not quite right either (this
  is not an endurance-training study) — recommend a new tag lane or explicit
  framing as **general strength/bone-health evidence, directly in this
  athlete's demographic, applied to a training-frequency/intensity
  question** — see `13-reds-energy-availability.md`'s own precedent of
  treating bone-health evidence as its own category. Confidence: high for
  safety and BMD/function outcomes in this specific population; note the
  intervention was **supervised** and the program's specific exercise list
  (barbell deadlift/squat/overhead press variants) is not itself a
  kettlebell protocol — the transferable claim is "heavy, twice-weekly
  loading is safe and effective for this demographic," not a specific
  kettlebell program.
  **Test:** if this athlete's strength program shifts toward heavier KB
  loading (e.g., heavier single bell, fewer reps) as a LIFTMOR-informed
  choice, track any new joint pain in wellness check-ins for the first
  2-3 weeks of the change — LIFTMOR's safety finding was under supervision,
  which this athlete's home-kettlebell setup does not replicate one-to-one.

- **~ Zhou Y., Wen K., Zhang X., Sun Y. (2025/2026)** — "Effects of
  resistance training on muscle mass, strength, and physical function in
  older women with sarcopenia: a systematic review and meta-analysis" —
  *Frontiers in Public Health*. **Verification:** title, authors, and
  journal confirmed via the publisher (Frontiers), PubMed, and PMC; marked
  `~` rather than `✓` because the search results returned **two different
  publication-year stamps for what may be the same or a closely related
  article** (2025 vs. 2026 across different listings) — the build agent
  should confirm the final published year/volume before citing.
  **Finding:** 12 RCTs, 518 older women with sarcopenia; resistance
  training significantly improved handgrip strength, gait speed, knee
  extension strength, and functional tests (Timed Up and Go, 30-second
  chair stand), but had **no significant effect on skeletal muscle mass
  index** — strength/function improve reliably; muscle *mass* gains are
  less consistent in this population.
  **Why it matters:** corroborates LIFTMOR's population-appropriateness
  point from a different angle (sarcopenia framing rather than bone-density
  framing) and supplies an honest caveat `07` should carry: resistance
  training's biggest payoff in an older-female population is
  strength/function, not necessarily visible muscle-mass change — useful
  for setting realistic expectations, not for dosing specifics.
  **Tag:** same framing as Watson above — general strength/aging evidence,
  directly in this athlete's demographic. Confidence: medium (meta-analysis
  of RCTs, but sarcopenia-diagnosed cohort, not necessarily matching an
  active masters athlete's baseline status, and the year/volume
  bibliographic gap above).

### Topic D — Concurrent training dosing and scheduling (beyond frequency)

- **✓ Wilson J.M., Marín P.J., Rhea M.R., Wilson S.M.C., Loenneke J.P.,
  Anderson J.C. (2012)** — "Concurrent Training: A Meta-Analysis Examining
  Interference of Aerobic and Resistance Exercises" — *Journal of Strength
  and Conditioning Research*, 26(8):2293-2307. **Verification:** full
  author list, journal, year, volume/issue/pages confirmed via the journal
  (LWW/Ovid), PubMed, and Semantic Scholar.
  **Finding:** pooling 21 studies (422 effect sizes), found a small-to-
  moderate interference effect of concurrent aerobic+resistance training on
  lower-body strength and power (effect sizes roughly -0.3 to -0.4);
  hypertrophy was not significantly affected. Interference was worse when
  running (vs. cycling) was the aerobic mode, and worse with high aerobic
  frequency/volume and same-day proximity to resistance training.
  **Tag:** `[ADAPTED: general-endurance]` Confidence: medium — this is the
  classic, most-cited concurrent-training meta-analysis, but it is not
  swim-specific and it predates more recent work (below) that softens its
  conclusions.

- **✓ Schumann M., Feuerbacher J.F., Sünkeler M., Freitag N., Rønnestad
  B.R., Doma K., Lundberg T.R. (2022)** — "Compatibility of Concurrent
  Aerobic and Strength Training for Skeletal Muscle Size and Function: An
  Updated Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis" — *Sports Medicine*,
  52:601-612. **Verification:** full author list, journal, year, volume/
  pages confirmed via the publisher (Springer), James Cook University's
  research repository, and ResearchGate.
  **Finding:** an update to Wilson (2012) finding **no significant
  differences in hypertrophy or maximal strength** between concurrent and
  strength-only training when frequency/volume are matched; **explosive/
  power strength gains may still be attenuated**, especially when aerobic
  and strength work are performed in the **same session**. Effects were
  largely independent of aerobic training type, concurrent-training
  frequency, training status, and age.
  **Why it matters for scheduling:** directly actionable beyond `07`'s
  current "not on pool days" placement rule (which is itself
  `Coach judgment:`, not cited) — this is real evidence that
  *same-session* pairing is the specific thing to avoid, more than same-day
  pairing per se, and that maximal-strength/hypertrophy goals (the
  rotator-cuff/scapular work `07` already prescribes) are relatively
  protected even under concurrent load, while any future explosive/power
  KB work (e.g., heavier ballistic swings for power) would be the more
  interference-sensitive category.
  **Tag:** `[ADAPTED: general-endurance]` Confidence: medium-high (2022
  systematic review/meta-analysis, updates and partially supersedes Wilson
  2012, but general-population concurrent-training literature, not
  swim-specific).
  **Test:** if this athlete's rotator-cuff/scapular strength work (already
  in `07`) stays separated from pool sessions by hours (not stacked in the
  same session), maximal-strength-style gains (per Schumann) should not be
  meaningfully compromised by pool volume; if a future explosive/power KB
  element were added and stacked same-session with a hard pool set, that
  is the specific combination this literature would predict to underperform
  relative to separating them.

- **✓ Huiberts R.O., Wüst R.C.I., van der Zwaard S. (2023, published
  2024)** — "Concurrent Strength and Endurance Training: A Systematic
  Review and Meta-Analysis on the Impact of Sex and Training Status" —
  *Sports Medicine*, 54(2):485-503. **Verification:** full author list,
  journal, year, volume/issue/pages confirmed via the publisher (Springer),
  Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam's research repository, and PubMed.
  **Finding:** examined sex-specific differences in strength, power,
  hypertrophy, and VO2max adaptations to concurrent training; training
  status (trained vs. untrained) moderates outcomes more consistently than
  sex does across the pooled literature.
  **Why it matters:** directly responsive to "does concurrent-training
  interference look different for a trained female masters athlete" —
  the honest answer per this review is that **training status matters more
  than sex** for concurrent-training outcomes, which argues against a
  female-specific interference concern being the dominant variable here
  (more useful as a myth-check than as a new dosing rule).
  **Tag:** `[ADAPTED: general-endurance]` Confidence: medium (systematic
  review/meta-analysis; general adult population, not masters- or
  swim-specific — the sex-comparison framing is the closest it gets to this
  athlete's specific question, and even that is indirect).

### Topic E — Heavy-load strength dosing for endurance performance (population-closer alternative to the review `07` already cites)

- **✓ Vikmoen O., Rønnestad B.R., et al. — two related trials:**
  "Strength training improves cycling performance, fractional utilization
  of VO2max and cycling economy in female cyclists" (*Scandinavian Journal
  of Medicine & Science in Sports*, 2016) and "Effects of Heavy Strength
  Training on Running Performance and Determinants of Running Performance
  in Female Endurance Athletes" (*PLOS ONE*, 2016), plus "Heavy strength
  training improves running and cycling performance following prolonged
  submaximal work in well-trained female athletes" (*Physiological
  Reports*, 2017). **Verification:** titles, Vikmoen/Rønnestad authorship,
  journals, and years confirmed via the publishers (Wiley, PLOS), PubMed,
  and PMC across all three.
  **Protocol shape (consistent across this research line, useful as a
  dosing template):** heavy strength training (progressive load building
  toward ~85-90%+ 1RM working sets, low rep counts in the 4-10 range),
  **low weekly volume, 2x/week**, added on top of normal endurance
  training, in **well-trained female endurance athletes** — a substantially
  closer population match to this athlete than the mixed-sex Rønnestad &
  Mujika (2014) review `07` already cites (which found strength benefits
  cycling economy more clearly than running economy, a nuance these
  follow-up trials in female athletes corroborate: cycling economy/
  performance improved consistently; running economy improvements were
  more equivocal).
  **Why it matters for Question 2's dosing question:** this is a
  concrete, reproducible **heavy-load** protocol shape (not the swimmer
  rehab studies' RPE/1RM-mixed dosing) specifically validated in female
  endurance athletes, giving `07`'s existing "does strength improve
  performance, not just prevent injury" section (currently `[ADAPTED:
  cycling/running] Confidence: low`) a stronger, closer-population citation
  to lean on if that section is ever revised — though it remains a
  performance question, separate from `07`'s injury-prevention framing.
  **Tag:** `[ADAPTED: general-endurance]` Confidence: medium (direct female
  endurance-athlete population — closer than the existing citation — but
  cycling/running, not swimming, and the mechanism-transfer caveat
  `07` already states for swimming's technique-dominated economy still
  applies).

## 3. What this supports for a new `library` file extending `07`

- **Kettlebell exercise-selection mapping** (Question 1's movement
  categories) can be written up with `Coach judgment:` tags citing the
  general-population EMG studies above (Lyons 2017 for ballistic
  swing/snatch/clean; Zebis 2013 for swing-as-posterior-chain; St-Onge
  et al. for Turkish get-up as genuine rotator-cuff work; Ellestad et al.
  2024 for carries as genuine anti-rotation trunk work) — **honest framing
  throughout: this is "here's what the movement demonstrably does
  mechanically," not "here's proof it helps swimmers."**
- **Overhead KB advisability** should cite Hogan et al. (2021) to correct
  the oversimplified "scapular dyskinesis -> avoid overhead" reasoning,
  paired explicitly with a pointer to `21-shoulder-health-and-load.md`'s
  actual return-to-sport criteria (pain ceiling, strength symmetry,
