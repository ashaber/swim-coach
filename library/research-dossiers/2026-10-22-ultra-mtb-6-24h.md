## Scope 1 — Pacing for 6-24 h MTB / ultra-cycling

**F1.1 Positive pacing (speed falls from lap 1) is the observed norm at
24 h solo MTB.** In 837 men and 157 women across eight World 24-h MTB
Championships (2012-2019), speed dropped from the first lap in all races
(P<.001), described as positive pacing. Mean distance 275.9 ± 67.2 km in
23.7 ± 1.1 h. The fastest cluster started slower than clusters 3-5 and
finished faster than clusters 4-5; first-lap speed was moderately and
inversely related to distance covered (r=-.358). Pacing did not differ
by sex. [#2, **ultra-cycling/MTB direct**, ⚠ abstract only; absolute
lap speeds not reported in what I read.] Implication: a fast opening
lap correlates with *less* total distance; the best riders started
relatively slower.

**F1.2 Road 24-h record: speed and power declined with distance.** One
rider, 896.173 km in 24 h, mean 37.34 km/h and 250.2 W; distance
correlated negatively with speed (r=-0.79) and power (r=-0.85); final
61.4 km in 1:23 h at 44.38 km/h (end-spurt). The paper is internally
inconsistent (abstract "linear", discussion "non-linear"). [#1,
**ultra-cycling direct**, ⚠ n=1 elite road record, drafting-free circuit.]

**F1.3 Multi-lap MTB: lap times were even, but within-lap speed varied
widely.** Six riders, 5 laps, no significant lap time or velocity
difference across laps, with large terrain-linked oscillation. [#6,
**MTB direct**, ⚠ short race, n=6; not an ultra.] Contrast: the athlete's
own 6-lap/7.2 h race showed lap slowdown of 2-4%, consistent with #2's
positive pacing at much longer durations.

**F1.4 Intensity falls over very long events.** In ten elite riders in
the 525 km Race across the Alps, HRaverage/HRmax fell from 0.86 at the
start to 0.66 at the end (a 23% decline); authors proposed an
"ultraendurance threshold" around 70% HRmax; ~53% of time was below 70%
HRmax and 3% above 90%. [#5, **ultra-cycling direct** (road, multi-hour
to ~20 h), ⚠ n=10, no comparison group, abstract via search summary.]
Ironman bike: power, cadence and speed all fell with lap number in six
well-trained triathletes (5 h 11 ± 2 min). [#8, **ADAPTED: tri**.]

**F1.5 Variability of pacing increases with duration (running).** In
937 time-limited ultramarathon finishers, 6 h finishers were fastest,
12 h oldest, and 24 h finishers had the most variable pacing (CV of
speed). [#3, **ADAPTED: running**, abstract.] Mean cycling speeds in
time-limited ultra-cycling: 6 h 26.2 (men)/23.5 (women) km/h; 12 h
23.2/21.0; 24 h 16.4/15.7. [#4, ultra-cycling direct, race-level
means; terrain/format mixed.] Practical reading: men's average speed
falls by ~11% from 6 h to 12 h and by ~29% from 12 h to 24 h
(arithmetic on #4 means) — the 24-h drop is far larger than a
physiological pace decay alone, so stops, sleep and night riding
dominate. This inference is **Coach judgment**; the paper doesn't
partition it.

**F1.6 General pacing theory.** Pacing profiles (even, positive,
negative, parabolic, variable); the review states optimal strategy for
ultra-endurance was unknown at the time. [#7, general.] No peer-reviewed
even-vs-variable *experiment* at 6-24 h in MTB was found.

**F1.7 Sleep strategy.** In a four-man RAAM team, sleep deprivation
worsened progressively, energy balance was negative, cyclists slept <1 h
per episode with long sleep latency, and *longer* rest breaks preserved
actual sleep and sleep efficiency better; authors recommend planning
sleep, not only average speed. [#11, ultra-cycling direct (road,
team-relay), ⚠.] A 2025 study of 26 amateurs found 25 h of sleep
deprivation raised RPE and worsened feeling scale at 40 min of cycling
and impaired vigilance, but did not change 20-min TT work; one recovery
night restored values. [#28 `~`, ADAPTED: general-endurance; lab, not an
ultra.] **No evidence found** comparing no-sleep vs short-nap strategies
in 24-h *solo MTB* or giving nap durations that preserve performance.

**F1.8 Pacing targets (%FTP/IF by duration): No peer-reviewed source
found.** The only quantitative anchors are HR-based (#5: ~0.66-0.70
HRmax late in a 525 km ride) and descriptive averages. Any
"IF ~0.6-0.7 for 12-24 h" band is **Coach judgment from coaching
practice**; for reference, the athlete's 7.2 h race was ~IF 0.63 by
NP/FTP arithmetic. Pit/stop strategy: **No evidence found** (only that
24-h speed is far below 12-h speed, #4).

**Context (not ultra):** XC Olympic races ~2 h at ~90% HRmax, >80% above
lactate threshold [#20] — a different regime; do not borrow its
intensity for 6 h+.

## Scope 2 — Training for 6-24 h events

**F2.1 Durability definition.** Time of onset and magnitude of
deterioration of physiological-profile characteristics during prolonged
exercise; proposed as a profiling dimension beyond fresh values [#12 `~`,
general endurance]. Greater relative intensity and longer duration of the
fatiguing protocol produce larger deterioration, so protocols must be
matched to the event and training status [#14, abstract].

**F2.2 Size of the effect in trained non-pro cyclists.** After 150 min of
moderate-intensity cycling in 13 well-trained riders (VO2peak 57.3
mL/kg/min, 12 h/wk), power at VT1 fell 211 → 198 W (~-6%) and 5-min TT
power 333 → 302 W (~-9%); the TT loss correlated with VT1-power
durability (rs=0.719), not with muscle carnosine, CS or complex I
[#13, cycling direct, ⚠ n=13]. In 14 endurance-trained amateurs, 20-min
power after 1,000 kJ fell ~6.5% in the more successful group vs ~12.5%
in the less successful; 5-min power and HR showed no group difference
[#16, ⚠ n=14]. Athlete's EF -7%, HR drift +7 bpm at -3% power over 7 h is
the same family of phenomenon (not a diagnosis).

**F2.3 Durability is not captured by fresh tests.** No significant
correlation with VO2max or relative FTP in 20 amateurs [#17 `~` ⚠]. In
49 pro-level road cyclists, higher-tier riders tolerated more prior
high-intensity work before MMP dropped (U23 from ≥5 kJ/kg above CP; Pro
Team/World Tour from 7.5 kJ/kg) [#15, ⚠ pro road, short report]. kJ
alone is a questionable stress/fatigue indicator because intensity of
prior work matters [#18 `~`].

**F2.4 Is durability trainable? No controlled trial found.** Searches
returned cross-sectional/observational work (#16, #17) and coaching
claims only. **No evidence found** that any specific intervention
(back-to-back days, late-ride intervals, fasted/low-CHO rides) raises
durability in cyclists. Treat "train under fatigue" as **Coach
judgment** (mechanistic plausibility + #13/#16 association with
success), not EVIDENCE.

**F2.8 FTP vs durability from a 24-h build (athlete question).** Race
target is a SOLO 24 h MTB around April 2027, with a Nov-Mar build.
Evidence on "what FTP gain to expect" from ultra-oriented training:
**No controlled study of FTP change from a 24-h/ultra build found.**
Closest data: (a) RAAM-prep team over 6 months of threshold-oriented
training gained CP +16.8 ±6.2% and VO2max +7.1% [#19, ⚠ n=4, no
control, athletes' starting training status unclear — not transferable
to a trained masters rider]; (b) durability is statistically separable
from fresh markers (no correlation with VO2max/relative FTP, #17 `~`;
fresh power similar between groups while fatigued power differs, Tour of
Alps summary seen only via a magazine article, not cited); (c) a thesis
10-week intervention reportedly improved durability without VO2max gain
and found high-intensity work not replaceable by volume [#32 `~` ⚠
summary only]. A supportable reading is: expect FTP to change little
and durability (late-ride power retained) to be the target, but this is
an inference from #17/#32/#16, not a measured result of an ultra build.
Any numeric FTP-gain forecast is **Coach judgment**; the athlete's
~2-3% season-to-season rise is the only anchor the repo itself holds.
Timing note for April: a Nov-Mar block gives ~20 weeks; no source
specifies the build length needed for durability change.

**F2.5 Training of ultra-cyclists.** The only data: a 2nd-place RAAM
team (4 athletes) over 6 months showed threshold-oriented TID (3-zone:
63/28/9% Z1/Z2/Z3; power-specific model 48/39/13%); VO2max +7.1 ±5.3%,
CP +16.8 ±6.2%, lactate-minimum power +10.3 ±4.1% [#19, ⚠ n=4,
no control]. **No evidence found** for long-ride length progression or
back-to-back-day volumes specific to 6-24 h cycling; no study of
night-riding practice or of lighting/drowsiness adaptation.

**F2.6 Masters.** Nearly half of ultra-cycling finishers (RAAM and
qualifiers) were masters (35-49), winners averaged ~37 y; DNF rate
~26-41% [#21, cross-sectional]. A 24-h draft-legal study put peak
performance age ~41 for men (secondary snippet; paper not individually
confirmed). **No evidence found** on masters-specific recovery or
durability training for 6-24 h MTB.

**F2.7 Taper before an ultra.** **No peer-reviewed ultra-event taper
study found.** Coaching sources (⚠ #29): 10-21 days for 10-24 h cycling
or 24-h runs, volume stepped down 20-30%/40-50%/60-70% at 3/2/1 weeks
with intensity held. The repo already holds Bosquet 2007 and Neary 2003
(see `2026-09-26-season-taper.md`); an ultra taper is an extrapolation
(ADAPTED: general-endurance), **Coach judgment**.

## Scope 3 — Fueling / hydration / sleep-deprivation physiology (12-24 h specifics)

**F3.1 CHO per hour.** Guidance of up to ~90 g/h (multiple-transportable
CHO, glucose:fructose ~2:1 or 1:0.8) for efforts >2.5-3 h [#22, #23,
secondary descriptions of #23]. 120 g/h raised exogenous oxidation
(1.51 vs 1.29 g/min, p=0.026) over 3 h at ~95% GET in 11 cyclists but
left endogenous oxidation unchanged (2.15 vs 2.20 g/min), i.e. no
glycogen sparing [#24, ⚠ lab 3 h, fasted]. Secondary sources say
performance benefit beyond 90 g/h is not consistently shown (not
verified in primary). **At 12-24 h** no study tests >90 g/h; at
sub-threshold ultra intensities, absolute CHO need is lower than the lab
cases — **Coach judgment**.

**F3.2 Energy deficit is large and expected.** A single 24-h road
ultracyclist (39 y, 84.2 kg) rode 22 h 22 min, 557 km, mean HR 131; est.
expenditure 15,533 kcal vs intake 5,571 kcal (deficit 9,915 kcal);
CHO 1,102 g (13.1 g/kg; ~49 g/h over 22.4 h by arithmetic); fluid 20.7 L
(862 mL/h, 34 mmol/L sodium); caffeine 231 mg [#9, ⚠ n=1, HR-regression
EE estimate]. RAAM team: daily energy balance negative throughout [#11].
Different from short events: intake is capped by gut/appetite, so the
plan is "keep the deficit sustainable," not "match expenditure"
(**Coach judgment**).

**F3.3 Sodium / hyponatremia.** In two 24-h MTB races (27 ultra-MTBers)
plus a 24-h run and a multi-stage MTB race, 3/53 (5.7%) finishers had
post-race EAH (1 MTB, 3.7%); plasma [Na+] fell significantly only in the
multi-stage race; in the two 24-h MTB races higher fluid intake was
positively related to performance (i.e., faster riders drank more)
[#10, ⚠ small, Czech events]. Consensus: drink to thirst, avoid
overdrinking [#27]. Optimal sodium g/h for 24 h MTB: **No evidence
found**.

**F3.4 GI issues.** Gut training (2 weeks, daily 1 h at 60% VO2max,
90 g/h vs 0 g/h) reduced GI symptoms and CHO malabsorption in 18 runners
[#25, ADAPTED: running, ⚠]; a 2022 follow-up reportedly found minor
effects of a very-high-CHO diet plus gut training (title seen only in
search listing, not verified). Systematic review of nutrition strategies
for GI symptoms in ultra athletes exists [#26]; findings not read.

**F3.5 Caffeine overnight.** Multiple 200 mg doses preserved psychomotor
vigilance vs placebo over 77 h total sleep deprivation in a military
study (secondary snippet, authors not retained → not cited as a
source); caffeine "cannot replace sleep". **No evidence found** of a
caffeine trial in ultra-cycling during sleep loss. Dose/timing for a
24-h MTB night is **Coach judgment**.

**F3.6 Body-mass change in 24-h MTB:** a Knechtle-group study of 49
24-h ultra-MTBers tracked body mass/hydration; abstract results were not
retrieved → **No evidence found** (in this pass) on typical mass change.

## Scope 4 — Analysis implications (what a coach can compute)

Only what sources support:

- **Within-race HR/power relationship:** relative HR fell across an
  ultra (0.86 → 0.66 HRmax, #5), whereas the athlete's 100 km showed HR
  *rising* at lower power; both are "intensity-vs-output drift" and the
  direction is context-dependent (heat, terrain, pacing). Source support
  for decoupling thresholds in 7-24 h events: **No evidence found**.
  Cardiac-drift literature found only as lab studies of 15-45 min and
  2-h cyclist efficiency (authors not retained) — not cited.
- **Lap-time/speed decay:** fraction of lap-1 speed retained by the
  last lap, and ratio of first vs middle vs last lap speed — the exact
  normalisation used in #2. Even between-lap pace is plausible in a
  ≤5-lap MTB race (#6).
- **Durability index:** compare MMP/power (e.g., 5-20 min best) after a
  given kJ (or kJ/kg) vs fresh (#13, #15, #16). Protocol-dependent
  (#14); relative drop after 1,000 kJ ~6.5% (better) vs ~12.5% (worse)
  is a cross-sectional anchor for 14 amateur riders only (#16, ⚠).
- **Intensity of prior work, not just kJ** (#18).
- **Efficiency factor trend (NP/HR by lap):** a coaching-practice
  metric; no peer-reviewed validation for ultra MTB found (**Coach
  judgment**).
