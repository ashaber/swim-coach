## Q1 — Taper length/depth for SHORT (≤1h) cycling or high-intensity events

- **✓ Neary P.J., Bhambhani Y., McKenzie D.C. (2003)** — "Effects of
  Different Stepwise Reduction Taper Protocols on Cycling Performance" —
  *Canadian Journal of Applied Physiology*, 28(4):576-587. Verified via
  the publisher (cdnsciencepub.com/NRC Research Press) plus independent
  secondary convergence (ResearchGate). 11 male cyclists, a **7-day**
  taper with three volume-cut magnitudes (30%/50%/80%) at fixed intensity
  (85% pretaper). The 50%-cut protocol produced a significant 5.4%
  improvement in simulated 20-km time trial (a ~25-30 min effort — good
  duration match for a CX race); 30% and 80% cuts did not reach
  significance.
  **[EVIDENCE: cycling]** A 7-day, ~50%-volume-cut taper (intensity held)
  is directly supported for a short/near-CX-duration TT effort — this is
  real evidence for a taper shorter than the general 8-14-day finding, not
  just an extrapolation from it. Confidence: medium (lab TT, not a
  technical mass-start CX race). **Test:** if power/RPE at a CX A-race
  reads flat despite a much-deeper cut than 50%, the taper overshot depth,
  not necessarily duration — pull volume back toward ~50%, not toward a
  longer window.

- **~ Neary P.J., Martin T.P., Reid D.C., Burnham R., Quinney H.A.
  (1992)** — "The effects of a reduced exercise duration taper programme
  on performance and muscle enzymes of endurance cyclists" — *European
  Journal of Applied Physiology and Occupational Physiology*, 65(1):30-36.
  Verified as a real paper (Springer + PubMed PMID 1505537), design
  confirmed (25 cyclists, 8-wk base, then 4-day taper vs. 8-day taper vs.
  4-day full-rest vs. continued training). Both the 4-day and 8-day taper
  arms improved performance/muscle-oxidative markers vs. continued
  training. Marked **~** rather than ✓ because the head-to-head
  4-day-vs-8-day *performance* magnitude (not just the enzyme/oxidative
  data) was not independently re-derived from primary text this pass —
  only confirmed via secondary summaries.
  **[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Confidence: low-medium — supports that a taper
  as short as 4 days is a viable floor, not that 4 days is provably
  equal to 8. **Test:** treat 4 days as a plausible minimum for a very
  short race, not a proven optimum; if a 4-day cut leaves the athlete
  flat, lengthen toward 6-7 days (per Neary 2003 above) before assuming a
  full 10-14 day taper is needed.

**Honest gap:** no cyclocross-, MTB-, or criterium-*specific* taper study
was found (searched directly this pass — same "genuinely thin"
conclusion `31-multi-race-season-periodization.md` already reached for CX
literature generally). Practitioner sources actively **disagree**:
several argue short/explosive disciplines need a *shorter* taper (less
aerobic staleness risk, faster peripheral-fatigue recovery); at least one
argues CX/crit/MTB need a *longer* taper for full neuromuscular
freshness/explosiveness. This is a real, unresolved practitioner
disagreement — `Coach judgment:`, not evidence, and should be labeled as
such if written into `31`.

**Recommended number for this athlete:** a 5-7 day taper with a ~50%
volume cut, intensity/frequency held, for the A-peak weekend — shallower
than the existing 2-week `TAPER_WEEKS_SHORT`/Bosquet-grounded full taper,
directly supported by Neary 2003. Confidence: medium.

## Q2 — Does a B-race taper cost fitness?

No study tests a "B-race taper" as its own named intervention, but two
sources directly answer "does a short reduction in training cost
fitness":

- **✓ Houmard J.A. (1991)** — "Impact of Reduced Training on Performance
  in Endurance Athletes" — *Sports Medicine*, 12(6):380-393. Verified via
  Springer + PubMed (PMID 1784880). Review conclusion: VO2max/max
  workload maintained for **10-28 days** with volume cuts up to 70-80%;
  athletic performance maintained or *improved* with 60-90% volume cuts
  over 6-21 days, **provided training frequency drops no more than ~20%**.
- **✓ Rietjens G.J., Keizer H.A., Kuipers H., Saris W.H. (2001)** — "A
  reduction in training volume and intensity for 21 days does not impair
  performance in cyclists" — *British Journal of Sports Medicine*,
  35(6):431-434. Verified via PubMed (PMID 11726481) + PMC full text.
  Direct cyclist population, 21-day reduced-volume-and-intensity window,
  no performance impairment.

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** A 1-week (7-day), ~25-50%-volume-cut B-race taper
sits comfortably inside both papers' no-fitness-loss windows (6-28 days,
up to 60-90% cuts). This gives the engine's existing `MINI_TAPER_WEEKS =
1` / Friel-only citation a genuine peer-reviewed backstop, not just a
practitioner opinion. Confidence: medium-high. **Test:** if repeated
B-race mini-tapers across a season correlate with a visible downward CTL
drift the athlete didn't intend, the frequency cut (not volume cut) is
probably too large — Houmard's own caveat (frequency drop ≤~20%) is the
lever to check first.

## Q3 — In-season minimum-dose maintenance (VO2max/threshold/strength)

- **✓ Rønnestad B.R., Hansen E.A., Raastad T. (2010)** — "In-season
  strength maintenance training increases well-trained cyclists'
  performance" — *European Journal of Applied Physiology*, 110(6):
  1269-1282. Verified via Springer + PubMed (PMID 20799042). **1x/week**
  strength maintenance (RM-based loading) through 13 weeks of competition
  season preserved thigh CSA/strength gains from the prep period with no
  negative effect on VO2max or cycling economy.
- **✓ Spiering B.A., Mujika I., Sharp M.A., Foulis S.A. (2021)** —
  "Maintaining Physical Performance: The Minimal Dose of Exercise Needed
  to Preserve Endurance and Strength Over Time" — *Journal of Strength and
  Conditioning Research*, 35(5):1449-1458. Verified via LWW/JSCR (DOI
  10.1519/JSC.0000000000003964) + PubMed (PMID 33629972). Review
  conclusion: **intensity is the key variable** — endurance adaptations
  are well-preserved despite large cuts to frequency/volume, as long as
  intensity is held; cutting intensity is what actually costs fitness.
- **✓ Bickel C.S., Cross J.M., Bamman M.M. (2011)** — "Exercise Dosing to
  Retain Resistance Training Adaptations in Young and Older Adults" —
  *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 43(7):1177-1187. Verified via
  ACSM/MSSE + PubMed (PMID 21131862). Strength gains maintained (younger
  adults) with training volume/frequency cut to as little as 1/3-1/9 of
  the original dose, intensity held.
- **✓ Hickson R.C., Rosenkoetter M.A. (1981)** — "Reduced training
  frequencies and maintenance of increased aerobic power" — *Medicine &
  Science in Sports & Exercise*, 13(1):13-16. Verified via ACSM/MSSE +
  PubMed (PMID 7219129). Classic result: VO2max gains from 6 d/wk training
  fully held at **2 d/wk** for 15 weeks, provided intensity/duration per
  session were unchanged.

**[ADAPTED: cycling]** for Rønnestad (direct cycling population, high
confidence); **[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** for Spiering/Bickel/Hickson
(cross-discipline populations, but converging and frequently cited).
**Concrete recommendation:** between B races, hold **~2x/week at full
intensity** for endurance/VO2max-threshold work and **~1x every 7-10
days** for strength — both are floors shown not to erode fitness, not
guesses. Confidence: high for the qualitative "intensity > frequency/
volume" principle; medium for the exact 2x/week number applied to CX
specifically (none of these trials used cyclocross racers). **Test:** if
an athlete's threshold power/pace visibly slides across a multi-week
B-race stretch at this dose, check intensity fidelity first (these papers
all assume intensity is genuinely held, not just "some riding happened").

## Q4 — CTL/weekly-TSS ramp-rate guidance: evidence or convention?

**Confirmed: convention only, no peer-reviewed evidence found.** Searched
directly this pass (TrainingPeaks' own blog series, Joe Friel's own site,
Alan Couzens' coaching blog). Every source, including Friel's own
material, describes the 5-8 CTL-points/week figure as derived from
**practitioner coaching experience and trial-and-error**, not a cited
study — one source explicitly frames it as individualized ("5-15/month,
more 'fragile' athletes on the low end") rather than a fixed law.
**~ Friel J.** — "The CTL Ramp Rate" (joefrieltraining.com) and
TrainingPeaks' "Why Ramp Rate is an Important Training Metric" —
practitioner/coaching-blog tier, not peer-reviewed.
**Label honestly:** `Coach judgment:` / practitioner convention, if this
number is ever written into a library file — do not tag `[EVIDENCE:
cycling]`. Also worth flagging in `31` or `24` if either file ever cites
this: the engine's own `WEEKLY_VOLUME_RAMP_CAP = 0.08` (`plan.py`) is a
**% change in session/weekly volume**, cited to `CLAUDE.md`'s own safety
rail, not to CTL points — the two "8"s are unit-incompatible and
coincidental; don't let a future edit imply cross-validation that doesn't
exist.

## Q5 — Race-day TSB target for short events: evidence beyond the existing practitioner citation?

**No new evidence found; the gap already flagged in
`22-injury-adapted-taper.md` stands.** Searched directly this pass
(TrainingPeaks/TrainerRoad/Roadman Cycling and related coaching-blog
sources) — multiple practitioner sources converge on a race-day TSB band
roughly in the same +5 to +25 territory `22`'s `RACE_DAY_TSB_BAND = {5,
25}` already cites (from a TrainingPeaks blog, medium confidence per that
file), with a couple of sources suggesting the lower end (+5 to +15) fits
shorter/single-day events specifically. But **every source found
explicitly states there is no research-validated universal TSB target**
— it's presented as coaching heuristic, individual- and
history-dependent. **Do not upgrade** `RACE_DAY_TSB_BAND`'s existing
confidence rating or citation on the strength of this search; the
honest state is: still practitioner convention, still medium confidence,
still worth an athlete-specific `Test:` (does this athlete's real race
performance track TSB the way the convention predicts) rather than a
literature-backed number.
