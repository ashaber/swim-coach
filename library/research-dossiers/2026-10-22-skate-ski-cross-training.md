# Skate-Ski Cross-Training for a Cyclist Research Dossier — feeds a future `library/43-skate-ski-cross-training.md`

> **Provenance note:** raw research input for a masters MTB/cyclocross
> athlete (FTP ~278 W, ~823 m elevation) planning to replace much of the
> Nov-Mar indoor bike base with cross-country **skate skiing** ahead of a
> solo 24-hour MTB race (~April 2027). **Not itself a citable library
> file** — for grounding claims, cite `library/reference_list.md` and the
> target library file(s) directly, never this dossier. Same convention as
> the other dated dossiers in this directory. 24-h MTB training/pacing/
> fueling is covered by a separate pass and is NOT repeated here.

Already in the repo, NOT re-researched: `library/20-cross-train-load-standardization.md`
(no-RPE/no-HR default-load case; it concluded no rigorous standard exists).
This dossier covers only what is skate-ski specific.

**Verification method:** every source below was located by web search on
title + author(s) (publisher/abstract/library-catalog records). No URL,
PMID, PMC or DOI is recorded, by design. Numbers are quoted only where an
abstract or record showed them; "abstract" means that is all that was seen.
No full texts were read.

**Source count this pass:** 18 ✓ · 6 ~ · 4 ⚠-only (several ✓/~ entries also carry a ⚠ caveat; counted under the first mark).

**Headline:** no study was found that trains cyclists with XC/roller skiing
and then measures cycling performance. Everything on "skiing → cycling"
transfer is therefore `ADAPTED` (from running/triathlon/arm-leg transfer
literature) or `Coach judgment`. That is itself the main finding.

## Area 1 — Transfer to cycling (highest priority)

**1a. Direct xc-ski→cycling evidence: none found.** Searches for XC/roller
skiing as cross-training for cyclists/MTBers returned no controlled study
and no case series with cycling outcomes. Practitioner sources (above) say
transfer to the bike is plausible but unproven and that the pedalling
pattern differs enough that "a period of adaptation" is needed. *Tag:
Coach judgment.*

**1b. Training transfer is modality-specific.** Millet et al. 2009 (#8)
conclude VO2max is specific to exercise modality and that, in triathletes,
transfer is likely larger running→cycling than cycling→running. Foster et
al. 1995 (#7): in 30 well-trained adults over 8 weeks, only extra
*running* raised running speed at 4 mmol/L lactate; the swimming group's
changes appeared in arm-crank tests. The secondary sources disagree on
whether swimming improved the 3.2-km time trial, so cite only the
lactate-speed point and flag the TT point as unresolved.
*Tag: ADAPTED: running+swimming/general-endurance.* Confidence: low-medium
(small 1995 study, different sports). **Test:** if bike FTP and 20-min
power do not hold after a ski block while ski-specific fitness rises, treat
that as the expected specificity result, not a plan failure.

**1c. Arm-trained fitness barely transfers to legs.** Systematic review
(#21): arm VO2peak rose in 21/29 interventions (+16.4%), but only 3 of 9
studies that tested untrained legs found a leg VO2peak gain (+9.3%); the
authors call transfer inconclusive. Holmberg et al. 2002 (#10, conference
abstract): upper-body-only peak VO2 was clearly lower than running or
diagonal skiing in elite skiers. Skate skiing is whole-body, so this is not
the same as isolated arm work; it only shows the arms add little central
stimulus alone and the legs likely carry the bulk of aerobic load at higher
intensity. *Tag: ADAPTED: general-endurance.* Confidence: low.

**1d. How much bike volume to retain bike-specific fitness.** The
detraining and reduced-training literature is endurance-general, not
cycling-vs-ski:
- Spiering et al. 2021 (#5, abstract): endurance performance maintained up
  to 15 weeks with as few as 2 sessions/wk or volume cut 33-66%,
  **provided intensity is kept**; the review explicitly says data are
  insufficient for athlete-specific recommendations. Hickson & Rosenkoetter
  1981 (#3): 6→2 sessions/wk for 5 wk held VO2max. Hickson et al. 1985 (#4):
  cutting intensity hurts more than cutting frequency or duration. Mujika
  2010 (#6) agrees: volume can drop if intensity stays.
- Mujika & Padilla 2000 (#1, #2): with *no* sport stimulus, trained
  athletes lose VO2max, blood volume and LT within weeks; losses are
  progressive after 4 weeks but stay above untrained values.

These studies largely reduce the *same* sport. Replacing bike with ski is
partly a "keep the aerobic engine, change the muscles" situation, so
carry-over of central adaptations is plausible; the peripheral,
pedalling-specific ones are the at-risk part. *Tag: ADAPTED:
general-endurance.* Confidence: medium for the intensity-protects-fitness
principle, low for any specific bike volume number.

**Coach judgment (not evidence) on dosing:** a defensible default is to
keep 1-2 short, *intense* bike sessions per week all winter (threshold or
VO2max intervals), consistent with #3-#6, and let skiing supply the
volume. Starting point only; individualise from FTP/power-curve tracking.

**1e. How fast bike power returns after the block; ski→bike phasing.**
**No evidence found** on the time-course of cycling power return after an
XC-ski block. The detraining reviews (#1, #2) and reduced-training reviews
(#3-#5) suggest central fitness is retained better than it is rebuilt, but
nothing supports a number of weeks. Phasing into April is `Coach judgment`:
(i) from roughly 8-10 weeks out, reintroduce bike volume progressively
while ski volume tapers, (ii) put 24-h-specific long rides, saddle time and
fueling practice in the last 6-8 weeks (the separate 24-h dossier owns that
content), (iii) use FTP re-tests and power-duration-curve checks at week 8
and week 4 as the data-driven gate. All `Coach judgment`.

**1f. What matters for MTB racing.** Impellizzeri et al. 2005 (#20, n=12):
in elite XC-MTB racers, threshold power/VO2 correlated with race time, not
VO2max or peak power. Implication (ADAPTED, weak): ski work that builds
aerobic capacity matters less than preserving bike-specific *threshold*
power and muscular endurance in the position.

## Area 2 — Skate-ski physiology vs cycling

**2a. Direct skate-ski-vs-cycling energy/HR comparison: none found.** No
paper compared roller/snow skate skiing with cycling at matched RPE or
power in the same athletes. What exists is ski vs *running*.

**2b. Sport-specific threshold HR: ski LTHR is lower than run LTHR in the
one lab study found.** Rundell 1996 (#9, n=7 elite biathlon women): both
lactate-threshold VO2 and HR were significantly lower in treadmill roller
skiing than in running, and ski-specific testing predicted ski race
results better than run testing. Direction vs *cycling*: not measured.
Millet et al. 2009 (#8) show threshold/VO2max also differ between cycling
and running, so a third, ski-specific number is expected. *Tag:
ADAPTED: running (ski vs run); cycling comparison = no evidence.*
Confidence: low (n=7, elite women, treadmill). **Test:** a 20-30 min
steady field effort on skis should give a ski LTHR; if it differs from the
bike LTHR by >5 bpm, use the ski value for ski sessions in load.

**2c. Muscle mass and the arms.** Skate skiing recruits legs, trunk and
poles. Holmberg 2002 (#10): upper-body-only peak VO2 is clearly lower than
whole-body skiing. A 2014 recreational-skier muscle-metabolism study (seen
only in a search summary, author unresolved) reports arm muscle metabolism
rises only marginally as intensity rises, with the legs taking up the added
work. Together this suggests that for a recreational skater the legs likely
drive the aerobic stimulus at high intensity, with arms/trunk setting
local-fatigue limits. *Tag: ADAPTED, confidence low*; partly ⚠ sources.

**2d. Economy and technique.** Millet et al. 1998 (#22): skilled skiers
self-select the most economical cycle rate in V2-alternate. A 2005 EJAP
study (#23, authors unresolved) found V2 costs more than V1 on moderate/
steep uphill at the same speed for national-team skiers. Both are skilled
cohorts; a novice's economy will be worse and noisier, so HR likely drifts
higher at a given speed early in the block. *Tag: xc-ski direct, applied
outside its population (skilled→novice) = low confidence.*

**2e. Intensity distribution used by XC skiers.** Seiler & Kjerland 2006
(#13, ~, n=11 junior men): about 75% of sessions below the first lactate
threshold zone, 15-20% well above, little in between; HR and sRPE agreed.
Sandbakk 2016 (#11): world-cup women did more low-intensity and moderate
volume than national-class peers. Sylta et al. 2014 (#14): a camp of 29
elite skiers sat ~96% of time in zone 1 by HR time-in-zone, vs ~87% of
sessions in zone 1 by session-goal; the method changes the answer.
*Tag: xc-ski direct, but elite/junior skiers, not masters recreational.*
Library use: a mostly-easy-with-some-hard winter shape is consistent with
what the existing cycling dossiers assume; do not import elite *volume*.

## Area 3 — Load accounting (ski-specific only)

- **No power, so HR or RPE only.** Foster et al. 2001 (#15): sRPE tracked
  HR-based load across modalities, but absolute sRPE scores ran higher than
  HR-based ones, so sRPE and TSS-like numbers are not interchangeable
  without calibration. *Tag: general-endurance (not ski).*
- **HR-based load needs the sport's own thresholds.** TRIMP lineage
  (Banister 1975, #16, secondary citation) is HR-reserve weighted. If a
  bike LTHR/HRmax is applied to ski HR data, #9 implies zones may
  mis-classify (ski LT HR lower than run LT HR in that study). Use a
  ski-specific LTHR. *Tag: ADAPTED: running.*
- **Zone-method choice matters.** Sylta et al. 2014 (#14): HR time-in-zone
  vs session-goal methods spread the same skiing very differently, with
  conversion factors reported (0.9/1.1 for zone 1; 3.0/0.33 for zones 2-3).
  Implication: a ski session with a few hard intervals is under-credited by
  pure time-in-zone HR. Consider session-RPE as a cross-check on hard days.
  *Tag: xc-ski direct (elite).*
- **Upper-body fatigue.** Local arm/trunk fatigue can end a session before
  legs/heart are stressed; HR then under-reports the cost. **No quantified
  evidence found**; `Coach judgment`: take the higher of the HR-based and
  sRPE-based load for ski days until calibrated.
- **Cross-sport conversion to bike TSS: no evidence found.** No published
  ski-TSS to bike-TSS equivalence. `library/20` already says no rigorous
  standard exists for the no-signal case; here at least HR and RPE exist.

## Area 4 — Practical / safety (short, source-supported only)

- **Learning curve (adult beginner V1/V2/V2-alt):** **No evidence found**
  on time-to-competence for adult novices. Evidence is only from skilled
  skiers (#22, #23). `Coach judgment`: expect several sessions of elevated
  HR/RPE at low speed while technique improves; judge early sessions by RPE
  and duration, not zones.
- **Injury patterns:** Back pain is common in elite skiers (64% of 87
  skiers, mostly diagonal-style, none from skating alone; #25 ⚠ summary).
  Kelsall & Finch 1996 (#26 ⚠): knee, arm/hand, ankle injuries most common;
  poor technique, poor tracks, equipment and overuse are risk factors.
  Clarsen/Bahr (#24): shoulder/low back are tracked overuse sites in
  Norwegian sports; skier-only numbers not seen. **Hip-flexor and shoulder
  overuse rates in recreational skate skiers: no evidence found.** Falls and
  knee/thumb ligament injuries (per #27) are a real acute risk for beginners.
- **Cold/altitude:** #28 (⚠) shows airway inflammation in elite skiers,
  cold dry air hypothesised. Altitude: studies seen were live-high/train-low
  in elite skiers; nothing on recreational skiing at ~800-1500 m. **No
  evidence found** for a practical rule; do not import.
- **Interference with strength:** Wilson 2012 (#19): interference grows with
  endurance frequency and duration and depends on modality. Rønnestad &
  Mujika 2014 (#17): heavy strength plus endurance is best supported for
  cycling performance. #18 tests this in trained cyclists with high
  endurance volume. *Tag: EVIDENCE: cycling (#17); ADAPTED for ski.* Coach
  judgment: keep 2 heavy leg sessions/wk, avoid stacking with hard ski
  intervals.

## Gaps — no evidence found

1. Any study training cyclists/MTBers with XC or roller skiing and
   measuring bike power or time trial.
2. Ski vs bike matched-effort HR/VO2/lactate comparison (ski LTHR vs bike
   LTHR in the same athlete).
3. Time-course of cycling power return after a non-bike block.
4. Minimum bike dose to retain bike-specific adaptations when another
   endurance sport supplies the volume.
5. Ski-TSS to bike-TSS equivalence, or any validated HR/RPE load model for
   skate skiing in recreational athletes.
6. Adult-beginner skate-ski learning curve.
7. Overuse-injury rates (shoulder, low back, hip flexors) for recreational
   skate skiers; most injury data are Nordic elite or classic-technique.

   titles and must not be cited as-is; #12 venue and #13 attribution should

**Engine implications (for a later build, not for this branch):**
- Add a per-sport LTHR (`lthr_ski`) in the athlete profile; load code should
  use the sport's own LTHR for hrTSS when sport is skate ski. Default must
  be explicit (e.g., "ski LTHR unset -> use RPE-based load and warn"), not
  silently the bike LTHR. Cite `library/43` once written.
- Prefer `max(hrTSS_ski, sRPE_load)` for ski days until the athlete has
  calibration data (Coach judgment constant; label it as such).
- `plan_check` advisory (never rejecting): flag when bike intensity sessions
  fall below 1/week for >4 consecutive weeks during a ski block (derived
  from #5, #6; advisory, confidence low).
- Advisory only: flag ski-heavy days adjacent to heavy leg-strength days.
