# Interval-Session Progression Research Dossier (2026-11-14)

> this dossier. Sources below are recorded as title + authors + year +
> journal only; no URLs or identifiers. Most were confirmed by a web search

Question: how should software progress interval sessions described by four
levers (rep duration, rest duration, reps per set, sets) within an intensity
zone?

## Summary (what is and is not supported)
1. No study I found directly compares the four levers (longer reps vs more reps vs shorter rest vs more sets) as progression steps. The lever ordering in the hypothesis is practice, not evidence.
2. For VO2max work, short intervals (30 s on / 15 s off, 3 x 13) beat effort-matched long intervals in two small cyclist trials; time at high VO2 is a function of on/off duration and relief intensity.
3. Total accumulated work at a moderately high intensity beat less work at higher intensity in one cyclist trial; ordering of interval duration across a block (increasing vs decreasing) made no difference at matched load.
4. For tempo/threshold progression (8 to 20+ min), step size, readiness signals and repetitions-before-progressing: no direct evidence found. HRV-guided scheduling has one small positive trial in recreational runners.
5. Recovery after threshold sessions may be slower than after VO2max sessions (one rower study); masters-specific evidence is thin and mixed. Swimming has CSS validation and one intermittent-at-CS study; USRPT evidence is weak.

**Source count:** 6 ✓ / 1 ~ / 16 ⚠ (23 total).

## 1. Which lever, in what order?

- **⚠ Rønnestad, Hansen, Vegge, Tønnessen, Slettaløkken (2015)** — "Short intervals induce superior training adaptations compared with long intervals in cyclists, an effort-matched approach" — *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports*. Caveat: recreational cyclists, 10 weeks, small groups, abstract-level. Short-interval group did 3 sets of 13 x 30 s on / 15 s off with 3 min between sets; long-interval group did 4 x 5 min work / 2.5 min rest, matched on effort. VO2max rose 8.7 +/- 5.0% (short) vs 2.6 +/- 5.2% (long).
- **⚠ Rønnestad, Hansen, Nygaard, Lundby (2020)** — "Superior performance improvements in elite cyclists following short-interval vs effort-matched long-interval training" — *Scand J Med Sci Sports*. Caveat: 9 per group, 3 weeks (3 sessions/week), abstract-level. Same session formats; 20-min mean power improved 4.7% (short) vs -1.4% (long).
- **⚠ Seiler, Jøranson, Olesen, Hetlelid (2013)** — "Adaptations to aerobic interval training: interactive effects of exercise intensity and total work duration" — *Scand J Med Sci Sports*. Caveat: I confirmed the paper and design, but the outcome statements below come from a secondary search summary, not the abstract itself; re-verify. 35 trained cyclists (VO2peak about 52 mL/kg/min), 7 weeks, twice weekly 4 x 4, 4 x 8 or 4 x 16 min at maximal tolerable intensity (about 94%, mid-90s, and 88-90% of peak HR; lactate roughly 13.2, 9.6, 4.9 mmol/L). Summary reported that 32 min of work near 90% HRmax gave larger gains than 16 min near 95%, with lower perceived exertion.
- **✓ Sylta, Tønnessen, Hammarström, Danielsen, Skovereng, Ravn, Rønnestad, Sandbakk, Seiler (2016)** — "The effect of different high-intensity periodization models on endurance adaptations" — *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. 63 cyclists, 12 weeks, 24 interval sessions, equal total load; one group increased interval duration across mesocycles, one decreased, one mixed. All improved 5-10% (40-min time trial power, peak power, VO2peak); no between-group difference.
- **⚠ Buchheit & Laursen (2013)** — "High-intensity interval training, solutions to the programming puzzle: Part I: cardiopulmonary emphasis" — *Sports Medicine*. Review, abstract-level. Describes HIT prescription as manipulation of up to nine variables (work/relief durations, intensities, modality, etc.); I did not read the detail on how to sequence them.
- **⚠ Laursen & Jenkins (2002)** — "The scientific basis for high-intensity interval training: optimising training programmes and maximising performance in highly trained endurance athletes" — *Sports Medicine*. Review, abstract-level: extra submaximal volume adds little in already well-trained athletes; HIT optimisation research was sparse.
- **⚠ Stöggl & Sperlich (2014)** — "Polarized training has greater impact on key endurance variables than threshold, high intensity, or high volume training" — *Frontiers in Physiology*. 48 mixed-sport endurance athletes, 9 weeks, HR-prescribed; polarized group gained most (VO2peak about +11.7%). Caveat: short, mixed sports, abstract-level.

**What this supports**
- At a matched effort, 30/15 organised as 3 x 13 produced larger gains than 5-min reps, in two cyclist trials (one elite).
- Accumulated duration near 90% HRmax can matter more than a higher intensity for less time (single trial, secondary-read).
- When total load is matched, whether rep duration rises or falls across a block did not change outcome; order is not a strong lever.

**What this does NOT support**
- Any ranking of "longer rep vs more reps vs less rest vs more sets" as progression levers. No source tests this.
- That starting short and lengthening is better than the reverse (Sylta says no detectable difference at matched load).
- Generalising short-interval superiority beyond 30/15 with 3 min between sets in 3 x per week blocks of 3 to 10 weeks.

## 2. Tempo, sweet spot, threshold, MLSS, critical power

- **✓ Baron, Dekerle, Robin, Neviere, Dupont, Matran, Vanvelcenaher, Robin, Pelayo (2003)** — "Maximal lactate steady state does not correspond to a complete physiological steady state" — *International Journal of Sports Medicine*. 8 trained male cyclists rode 30 min at MLSS power; lactate and gas exchange were stable from minute 10 to 30, but blood gases/pH, respiratory rate and HR drifted.
- **⚠ Fontana, Boutellier, Knöpfli-Lenzin (2009)** — "Time to exhaustion at maximal lactate steady state is similar for cycling and running in moderately trained subjects" — *European Journal of Applied Physiology*. 15 moderately trained men (not highly trained), abstract-level; time to exhaustion at MLSS did not differ between cycling and running. I did not retrieve the number.
- **⚠ Jones, Vanhatalo, Burnley, Morton, Poole (2010)** — "Critical power: implications for determination of VO2max and exercise tolerance" — *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. Only the bibliographic record and a one-line description were seen: time to exhaustion is a hyperbolic function of power with asymptote CP and curvature constant W'. No session-dose findings read.

**What this supports**
- MLSS/threshold power is sustainable for at least 30 min in trained cyclists, though not physiologically "steady" (drift continues), so late-rep difficulty is expected.
- Time-limit at MLSS is a real, measurable quantity that varies between individuals (the abstract-level record implies this; number not retrieved).

**What this does NOT support**
- A typical or maximal tolerable minutes-in-zone per session for tempo, sweet spot or threshold, or any 8 to 20+ min rep progression. I found no study of rep-length progression.
- How rest length changes the response to broken threshold work (e.g. 3 x 3 min with short recoveries). My search for this returned no matching study; the claim that short recoveries keep lactate/VO2 elevated is unverified here.

## 3. VO2max intervals

- **⚠ Seiler & Hetlelid (2005)** — "The impact of rest duration on work intensity and RPE during interval training" — *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. 9 well-trained male runners, treadmill, 6 x 4 min with 1, 2 or 4 min rest, self-paced. Speed slightly higher at 2 min than 1 min rest, no further rise at 4; work VO2 slightly higher at 2 min; peak RPE slightly lower at 4 min; self-selected rest averaged about 118 s. Running, n=9, abstract-level.
- **⚠ Billat, Slawinski, Bocquet, Demarle, Lafitte, Chassaing, Koralsztein (2000)** — "Intermittent runs at the velocity associated with maximal oxygen uptake enables subjects to remain at maximal oxygen uptake for a longer time than intense but submaximal runs" — *European Journal of Applied Physiology*. 8 long-distance runners, acute comparison; intermittent work at vVO2max gave more time at VO2max than continuous strenuous running. Authors state results do not speak to chronic adaptation. Abstract-level.
- **⚠ Midgley & McNaughton (2006)** — "Time at or near VO2max during continuous and intermittent running" — *Journal of Sports Medicine and Physical Fitness*. Running review, abstract-level. Abstract guidance: work about 90-105% vVO2max, relief between 50% vVO2max and lactate-threshold velocity, work and relief of 15 to 30 s; authors say more experimental work is needed before firm recommendations.
- **⚠ Bacon, Carter, Ogle, Joyner (2013)** — "VO2max trainability and high intensity interval training in humans: a meta-analysis" — *PLoS ONE*. 37 studies, 334 subjects, mostly not highly trained; pooled VO2max gain 0.51 L/min; a 9-study longer-interval subset had larger gains (about 0.8-0.9 L/min). Not a work:rest dose-response.
- **⚠ Billat (2001)** — "Interval training for performance: a scientific and empirical practice. Special recommendations for middle- and long-distance running. Part I: aerobic interval training" — *Sports Medicine*. Existence confirmed; content not read; no findings attributed.
- **~ Rønnestad & Hansen (2016)** — an interval-protocol study at maximal aerobic power with a 2:1 work:recovery ratio in well-trained cyclists — *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*. Authors are real and work in this area; exact title not confirmed (seen only as a search-result description), no findings attributed.
- The Rønnestad 2015 and 2020 papers in section 1 also bear on this section.

**What this supports**
- Short work/relief durations (15-30 s) with relief well below work intensity increase time near VO2max (running, acute; review-level guidance).
- Rest around 2 min was a reasonable balance for 4-min reps in runners (self-paced).
- Organising 30/15 into sets (13 reps, 3 min between sets) is the protocol actually tested in cyclists; the reason for the set break (an extended recovery to allow repeating the block at intensity) is my reading, not a finding.

**What this does NOT support**
- Starting at 15/15 and lengthening to 40 s. No study of that progression was found; 15 s to 30 s evidence is cross-sectional, between protocols.
- Passive vs active recovery effects: only the Midgley abstract guidance (relief 50% vVO2max to LT) was seen; no direct comparison read.
- A defensible "reps per set" or "number of sets" progression number beyond 13 x 3 as used in the trials.

## 4. Step size and readiness signals

- **⚠ Vesterinen, Nummela, Heikura, Laine, Hynynen, Botella, Häkkinen (2016)** — "Individual endurance training prescription with heart rate variability" — *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. 40 recreational runners; HRV-guided group did fewer moderate/high-intensity sessions (13.2 vs 17.7) and improved 3000 m time more; VO2max rose in both. Recreational runners, abstract-level.

**What this supports**
- Morning-HRV-gated scheduling of hard sessions was at least as good as a fixed plan in one small trial.

**What this does NOT support**
- Any figure for tolerable session-to-session increase in time-in-zone or rep length, the number of successful repetitions before progressing, or thresholds for RPE-at-dose, HR recovery or completion rate as readiness signals. **No evidence found; this is practice only.** Rushall-style and TrainerRoad-style "progression levels" are practice, not studies.

## 5. Recovery between hard sessions

- **⚠ Holt, Plews, Oberlin-Brown, Merien, Kilding (2019)** — "Cardiac parasympathetic and anaerobic performance recovery after high-intensity exercise in rowers" — *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance*. 13 highly trained rowers (10 male), three interval sessions (threshold, glycolytic, VO2max) one week apart, tracked HRV, HR recovery and modified Wingate power out to 72 h. Short-term responses were similar; post-submaximal HRV had the longest recovery course; threshold sessions recovered slowest overall, attributed to session duration. Rowing, abstract-level.

**What this supports**
- Longer-duration threshold sessions may need as long or longer to recover from than shorter high-intensity sessions, so a time-in-zone increase can raise recovery cost.

**What this does NOT support**
- A general 24/48/72 h rule for cyclists; cycling-specific time-course studies were not found. Lay claims (24-48 h, 48-72 h) seen in non-peer-reviewed sources are not used.

## 6. Masters athletes

- **⚠ Hottenrott, Möhle, Feichtinger, Ketelhut, Stoll, Hottenrott (2022)** — "Performance and recovery of well-trained younger and older athletes during different HIIT protocols" — *Sports*. Abstract/secondary summary: recovery after HIIT did not differ by age group and lactate kinetics were similar; the paper notes earlier studies, where older athletes had lower performance levels, found slower recovery.

**What this supports**
- Age alone may not slow recovery once performance level is matched (one study).

**What this does NOT support**
- Any age-specific change to rep, rest, set or step-size rules. The older-athlete evidence base on HIT is small; do not encode an age modifier as evidence-based.

## 7. Swimming analogue

- **✓ Wakayoshi, Ikuta, Yoshida, Udo, Moritani, Mutoh, Miyashita (1992)** — "Determination and validity of critical velocity as an index of swimming performance in the competitive swimmer" — *European Journal of Applied Physiology*. 9 trained college swimmers in a flume; critical velocity from slope of distance vs time (mean 1.166 m/s), proposed as an endurance index.
- **✓ Dekerle, Brickley, Alberty, Pelayo (2010)** — "Critical swimming speed and critical stroke rate in intermittent aerobic exercise" (title as indexed; verify exact wording) — *Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport*. 9 competitive male swimmers; 10 x 400 m at critical speed with 40 s recovery kept blood lactate stable while RPE rose. Abstract-level; I did not find time-at-VO2max data.
- **✓ Nugent et al. (2019)** — "Ultra-short race-pace training (USRPT) in swimming: current perspectives" — *Open Access Journal of Sports Medicine*. Systematic review; 15 studies screened and excluded as not truly USRPT, authors advise caution and call for RCTs. First-author initials and full list not confirmed.
- **⚠ Rushall** — coaching bulletins (not peer reviewed) advocating USRPT with intensity, not volume, as the driver; cites Mujika and Sperlich. Advocacy source; no trial data attached.
- Acute USRPT studies (national swimmers, 2023; age-group swimmers) report lower lactate and fatigue than volume-matched sets; acute only, not read in full, not counted as sources.

**What this supports**
- CSS/critical velocity is a validated index; broken sets at CS with short rest can keep lactate stable (rest 40 s, 400 m reps).
- USRPT is an unproven, contested contrast approach.

**What this does NOT support**
- Evidence for how to progress rep distance, send-off interval or total set distance. Not found.

## Implications for a lever-based progression model

| Lever | Zone family | What evidence supports | Confidence | Defensible number? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rep duration | Tempo/threshold | Nothing on rep-length progression; MLSS sustained 30 min in trained cyclists (Baron 2003) | none (progression), medium (30 min is tolerated) | No. 8-20 min = coach judgment. |
| Rep duration | VO2max | 30 s short reps beat 5-min reps at matched effort (Rønnestad 2015, 2020); 15-30 s favoured for time near VO2max (Midgley) | medium | 30 s on:15 s off defensible; 15 to 40 s lengthening = coach judgment. |
| Rest duration | Tempo/threshold | None found | none | No. |
| Rest duration | VO2max | About 2 min rest for 4-min reps (Seiler & Hetlelid, runners); short intervals use relief about 50% vVO2max to LT (Midgley) | low-medium | 15 s, 120 s, with sources; rest-shortening steps = coach judgment. |
| Reps per set | Tempo/threshold | None | none | No. |
| Reps per set | VO2max | 13 reps in tested 30/15 protocol | low | 13 only. 6 to 8+ start is coach judgment. |
| Sets | Both | 3 sets of 13 with 3 min between (Rønnestad); 4 x 4/8/16 min (Seiler 2013) | low | 3 sets (VO2max, cyclists) only. |
| Total time in zone | Both | Accumulated time near 90% HRmax mattered (Seiler 2013, secondary read); interval ordering irrelevant at matched load (Sylta 2016) | low-medium | No per-step rate. Block totals in the trials only. |

**Working-hypothesis points**
- *Within a zone, total time in zone grows across a block:* partly supported (accumulated duration matters), but no growth-rate evidence; refined to "total load matters, ordering does not."
- *Duration up, rest down:* not confirmed; the evidence neither tests nor contradicts it.
- *Tempo to 2 x 20 min threshold path:* unsupported and uncontradicted; no study.
- *VO2max start short and lengthen on-time:* partly contradicted in spirit: short intervals (30/15) were themselves the superior tested protocol, not a stepping stone to longer reps.
- *Then add reps, then sets:* consistent with the tested 3 x 13 structure, but order is practice.

## Research still needed
- Searches for broken-threshold recovery-length studies (second attempt) again found nothing.
- Direct comparison of reps vs rest vs sets progression at matched load in trained cyclists.
- Broken-threshold recovery-length studies (lactate, VO2, time in zone).
- Time-to-exhaustion at FTP/CP in trained cyclists with numbers (Fontana number not retrieved).
- Read full texts of Seiler 2013 (VO2max outcomes), Jones 2010, Buchheit & Laursen 2013, Billat 2001.
- Safe session-to-session increase and readiness signals (session RPE at dose, HR recovery, HRV).
- Cycling-specific recovery time course after threshold vs VO2max sessions; masters-specific cycling data.
- Swimming: rest/send-off/rep-distance progression trials; USRPT training trials.
