# Improving cycling starts: technique, gear and practice

See `39-cycling-start-analysis.md` for how a start is read and for the finding -> cause -> practice cue
table the analyzer's `cue_key` points at (its section 7); this file is the technique and training
evidence behind those cues. Citations marked "in 39" are listed there.

**Sport scope: `bike`.** **UNREVIEWED**, pending human review. No intervention study on **cyclocross**
starts exists; findings come from track, BMX and lab sprints, so every adapted claim is `Confidence: medium`
at best. Practitioner advice is labeled as such and is not evidence.

## 1. Technique determinants of the launch

**Standing, crank angle, hands. [ADAPTED: cycling] Confidence: medium.**
Padulo et al. (2014), 8 amateurs tested in the lab and on the track, plus
59 professionals observed: standing gave ~10% more initial speed and
acceleration over the first 5 m than seated; a starting crank angle of
**71°** (lead pedal forward of top, near "2 o'clock") beat 47°; hands on
the upper bar helped. Standing + 71° gave ~35% more acceleration than
seated + 47°. Professionals tended to adopt these settings on their own.
**Test:** label reps by start style (seated/standing) and compare 0–3 s
mean power; standing should be higher.

**Lead-leg force on the first stroke. [ADAPTED: cycling] Confidence:
medium.** Lead-leg torque predicts early splits (Kordi & van Rijswijk
2024, in 39); higher effective pedal force gives a faster first stroke
(Janssen & Cornelissen 2017, in 39). In BMX, set position matters: a
"back" set position was faster, and the second-crank weight-transfer phase
separated world-class from elite riders (Grigg 2019, doctoral thesis).
Bertucci & Hourde (2011): lab power tests explained 41–66% of BMX track
time from 5–75 m but **nothing** correlated with the first 5.2 m, so the
authors suggest the first metres depend on maximal force, rate of force
rise, or technique. **Test:** if launch rate (39, "What this means for the analyzer") is poor but
6-s peak power is normal, suspect technique, not capacity.

**Body position once up. [EVIDENCE: cycling] Confidence: medium.** Merkes,
Menaspà & Abbiss (2020), 11 recreational road cyclists: forward-standing
and traditional standing sprint positions gave similar power, cadence and
torque. Don't spend practice time on fine torso position after the launch.

**Clipping in / one-foot start.** No research located. Practitioner
sources (Overton 2022, Myerson 2017): clipped foot at ~2 o'clock, other
pedal level/ready; push off the ground with the free foot while driving
the clipped pedal; aim to clip in within the first revolution. This
agrees with Padulo's 71° crank finding. **Coach judgment:** a missed clip
shows as a stalled or flat first 1–2 s with low cadence, then a late
launch.

## 2. Gear selection and shifting in the start

**[ADAPTED: cycling] Confidence: medium.** A bigger launch gear raised peak
power without slowing time to peak (Rylands et al. 2017, in 39); peak
power came near 100 rpm, fastest time to peak near 120 rpm (Rylands et al.
2016, in 39). **No study of shifting during a start was found.**
Practitioner advice: on pavement pick a bigger gear, on grass/uphill an
easier one (Overton 2022); Myerson (2017) suggests big ring and one cog off
the easiest (2×), or 2–3 cogs down from the easiest on 1×, then shift up
as you accelerate; ease pedal pressure for half a stroke at each shift (Liv;
Matheny, in 39). **Coach judgment:** choose the gear where cadence passes
~90–110 rpm within ~3 s; shift once cadence is past ~110, never in the
first 2 strokes. **Test:** on reps with gear data (Di2/AXS), the bigger
of two launch gears should give a higher peak without a longer time to
peak; if time to peak lengthens, the bigger gear is too big for this rider.

## 3. Training that improves sprint/launch capacity

**Short maximal sprints from standstill. [EVIDENCE: cycling] Confidence:
medium-high.** Kristoffersen et al. (2019), 28 competitive cyclists, 6
weeks added to normal endurance training, 2×/week: 3 sets × 4 maximal
**seated standstill-start** sprints (4–8 s, progressing), 2 min active
recovery between reps, 5 min between sets, resistance set for peak power
at 110–120 rpm. Versus heavy strength training: 6-s sprint +4.7% (fresh),
+6.1% (after prolonged cycling), 30-s +3.7%, 55-m sprint on own bike
+4.3% in favour of sprint training; strength training improved half-squat
1RM more (+9.3% vs −3.9%). Adaptations were specific.

**Heavy strength vs on-bike torque work. [EVIDENCE: cycling] Confidence:
medium.** Barranco-Gil et al. (2025, RCT, well-trained men, 10 weeks):
full squats and on-bike high-resistance low-cadence efforts, matched for
sessions/sets/reps, gave similar Wingate gains (+4.1% and +4.3% vs −4.9%
in controls) and similar muscle-size gains. On-bike low-cadence work is a
reasonable substitute for gym work for a cyclist who won't lift.

**Strength links to the start. [ADAPTED: cycling] Confidence: medium.**
Stone et al. (2004): isometric mid-thigh-pull maximum strength correlated
strongly with standing-start sprint times and coach ranking (50 cyclists,
local to international). Gross & Gross (2019), 12 elite BMX: squat-jump
force (F0) vs start torque r = 0.77; jump Pmax vs start Pmax r = 0.85, but
high-velocity qualities didn't transfer, so test on the bike too. These
are correlations, not training effects. **Test:** after a strength block,
an athlete's start peak and 0–3 s power should rise more than 30-s mean
power does.

**Rate of torque development is measurable.** Connolly et al. (2023): RTD
was most reliable averaged over downstrokes 2–3 in a ≥ 5-s sprint against
heavy resistance, not over 1 s. **Coach judgment:** don't grade launch
off the first stroke alone; use 1–3 s.

**Reaction-time drills don't transfer.** Grigg (2019): 2 weeks/14 sessions
of BMX reaction training improved device reaction time but not ramp start
time (n = 4 vs 5 controls; weak). Practise the full start, not reflexes.

## 4. Structuring start practice

No controlled study of start-practice structure was found. **[ADAPTED: cycling]** Kristoffersen's 3×4 × 4–8 s, 2 min / 5 min rest, 2×/week is the
best evidence-backed template for maximal starts (Confidence: medium;
**Test:** start peak and 0–5 s mean power should rise ~3–6% over 6 weeks).
Recovery spacing per 39 section 4 (≥ 3 min for 10–20-s efforts). Practitioner
templates (not evidence): 5 × 20-s race starts (Overton 2022); stop with
one foot down then 15-s sprint (Myerson 2017); clip-in practice at every
stop sign, "100 reps a week for 4 weeks" (Overton). **Coach judgment:**
one technique cue per session; review each rep's trace (time to peak,
first-shift dip) before the next; stop the set when peak falls > ~10% from
the set's best.
