# Dressing for temperature on the bike (what to wear, layers, start feel, device temperature)

See `00-conventions.md` for the tagging scheme and `reference_list.md` for citations. Companions:
`42-ultra-mtb-endurance.md` (overnight events), `44-tire-pressure.md`, `45-bike-race-setup.md`.

**Sport scope: `bike`.** **UNREVIEWED**, pending human review. Written for a masters amateur
cyclist. **Evidence is thin here:** there are peer-reviewed studies on temperature and cycling
performance and on cold physiology, but none tests cycling clothing choices, "start cold" advice or
temperature charts. Every chart and rule of thumb is practitioner material and is labelled so.
**Individual variation is large, and the athlete's own record of what worked beats any chart.** A
chart is a starting point only. Temperatures are given in both F and C.

## 1. What peer-reviewed work supports (UNREVIEWED)

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Performance follows an inverted U with air temperature. Eight men cycled to
exhaustion at about 70% VO2max at 3.6, 10.5, 20.6 and 30.5 C (38, 51, 69, 87 F): time was shortest
at 30.5 C (51.6 min) and longest at 10.5 C (93.5 min) (Galloway and Maughan (1997); n=8, no clothing
manipulation). Field data from 74 professional cyclists showed a reverse-U with drops of roughly
9-18% at cold and hot extremes (Valenzuela et al. (2022); many confounders). Confidence: medium.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Heat impairs aerobic performance and skin temperature is part of
it: impairment begins above about 27 C (81 F) skin temperature, with roughly 1.5% further loss per
extra 1 C (Sawka et al. (2015)). A consensus statement names heat acclimatisation, by repeated
exercise-heat exposures over 1-2 weeks, as the most important intervention (Racinais et al. (2015)).
Confidence: medium (general heat physiology, not cycling kit).
Test: log felt heat and pace in the first hot rides of a season versus after 1-2 weeks of exposure.
`Coach judgment:` in heat, dress to maximise evaporation and shade, and acclimatise; do not rely
on kit.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Cold impairs endurance when clothing is inadequate. Ten men cycled
to exhaustion after 30 min in 22 C or 0 C (32 F) air: skin cooling with inadequate clothing cut
endurance by about 31% (borderline significance), and mild hypothermia (core down 0.5 or 1.0 C)
cut it by about 61-72% (Wallace et al. (2024); ten males, rest-then-ride, not a race start).
Confidence: low-medium. It supports "avoid being chilled" as the goal and does not test "start
cold". Test: note whether a ride started chilled felt harder at the same power than the same
ride started comfortable.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Cold causes skin vasoconstriction and shivering; size, sex,
fitness and thermoregulatory fatigue modify the response (Castellani and Young (2016)). Finger
rewarming typically begins 5-10 min after local cold exposure and is almost absent during
hypothermia (Daanen (2003)). Hands and feet lose first, and fatigued or under-fuelled riders lose
more. Confidence: medium (review papers, not cyclists specifically). Test: log when hands and feet
go cold relative to the core on a ride, and whether late-ride fatigue brings it on sooner.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Wind chill at riding speed. A computer simulation of a cyclist
found convective heat transfer scales with wind speed to a power of about 0.84, highest for legs,
arms, hands and feet and fairly insensitive to riding position (Defraeye et al. (2011); a model,
not a clothing test). Confidence: low-medium. Test: log felt temperature on descents versus flats in
the same kit.

**Computed, not cycling studies.** The US National Weather Service wind-chill formula is a face
model with no sun, valid at or below 50 F with wind over 3 mph, and says nothing on clothing. Applying
it (our arithmetic, not published by NWS): 50 F (10 C) at 20 mph (32 km/h) feels about 44 F (6 C);
40 F (4 C) at 15 mph (24 km/h) about 32 F (0 C); 30 F (-1 C) at 15 mph about 19 F (-7 C). NWS notes
bright sun may raise wind chill by 10-18 F. Use these as a guide to exposed hands, face and ears on
descents, not whole-body kit.

**Wet and cold.** The Wilderness Medical Society hypothermia guideline, as summarised by the AAFP
(Pyzocha (2020)), covers treatment of hypothermic patients, not racing. `Coach judgment:` wet kit
loses insulation, and a descent plus fatigue plus night makes stopping or reaching shelter the
right call. For ultras, carry a dry insulating layer in a waterproof bag and an emergency blanket.

**Intensity changes the answer (`Coach judgment:`).** Race intensity makes far more heat than Z2:
start thinner for a 45-min CX race, add a windproof for descents on a 4 h Z2 ride, and plan for
lower heat output and falling temperatures overnight in an ultra; the same temperature feels
colder at 3 a.m. than at noon. A pilot study of professional cyclists in cold conditions concluded
performance was limited by weather and advised warm-up, hydration and clothing strategies (Riera et
al. (2021)).

## 2. "Feel cool at the start" (UNREVIEWED)

The practitioner rule is that if you are comfortable standing still in your kit you are
overdressed (Roadman Cycling, 2026). **It is practitioner-only: no study tests it.** The mechanism
is plausible, since sweat-soaked clothing chills later, but Wallace et al. (2024) shows excess
chilling also harms. `Coach judgment:` apply it for hard efforts up to about 1 h, or where the first
minutes are uphill or fast; do not apply it before a long descent, an easy 4 h ride starting cold,
or an overnight start. Test: log start feel, felt temperature at 15 min, and whether you stopped
to add or remove layers.

## 3. CX: warm-up kit versus race kit

Practitioner advice, no temperature thresholds (TrainingPeaks, Boynton (2014); Cyclocross Magazine,
Mayhew (2018)): warm up in shelter in warm clothing; wear warm-up clothes to the line and remove them
as start time nears, with a helper taking them; use zippers and peel-down arm warmers to regulate
heat; start the race in clean, dry clothes. In wet conditions leg warmers can hold a lot of cold
water, so bare legs with non-warming embrocation as a water barrier may be better; shoe covers keep
feet clean and dry at the line. Bring a dry change for afterward. `Coach judgment:` swap-kit timing
is individual, so log what you wore.

## 4. Published chart (practitioner, not validated)

Roadman Cycling (2026) gives Celsius bands only; F added here. A first guess, not a prescription:

| Air temperature | Kit |
|---|---|
| Above 30 C (86 F) | light short sleeve, white cap |
| 20-25 C (68-77 F) | short sleeves and bibs, arm warmers carried |
| 15-20 C (59-68 F) | short sleeves plus arm warmers, knee warmers, gilet |
| 10-15 C (50-59 F) | long sleeves or arm warmers, leg warmers, gilet, light full-finger gloves, toe covers |
| 5-10 C (41-50 F) | thermal long sleeve, fleece tights, wind layer, insulated gloves, overshoes, neck gaiter |
| 0-5 C (32-41 F) | thermal jacket, thickest tights, heavy gloves, neoprene overshoes, thermal skull cap |
| Below 0 C (32 F) | add chemical toe warmers, face covering, foil blanket |

The chart ignores intensity, wind, wet, body size and the person's tolerance.

## 5. Extremities and personal log

`Coach judgment:` cover hands, feet, ears and head first, since they cool first and have high
convective loss and slower rewarming (Defraeye et al. (2011); Daanen (2003)); layer rather than
thicken. Cycling-specific evidence on gloves, booties or head covers was not found.

Body size, sex, fitness and fatigue (Castellani and Young (2016)) and diet, alcohol, altitude, age
and stress (Daanen (2003)) all change cold response, which is why a personal record beats a chart.
`Coach judgment:` log date, device temperature, wind, rain, kit, effort, felt temperature at 15 min
and at the end, and any change made. After about 10 entries, the athlete's own chart outranks any
published one.

## 6. Device temperature

Per Garmin Edge manuals (manufacturer), the device may read higher than actual air temperature in
direct sunlight, when held in the hand, or while charging from an external battery pack, and it
needs time to adapt after large temperature changes. `Coach judgment:` a wrist watch also reads high
from body heat (no source verified). Mount the head unit in shade, keep any external sensor away
from the body, discount sunny readings by several degrees, and cross-check against a shaded
thermometer or a weather service.
