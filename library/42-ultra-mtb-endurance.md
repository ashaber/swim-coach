# Ultra MTB endurance (6-24 h racing: pacing, durability, fueling, sleep)

See `00-conventions.md` for the tagging scheme and `reference_list.md` for full citations. Drafted
from `library/research-dossiers/2026-10-22-ultra-mtb-6-24h.md` (raw input, not itself citable).
Companions: `23-cycling-training.md` (power zones, FTP), `24-cycling-periodization-intervals.md`
(Seiler (2010)), `31-multi-race-season-periodization.md`, `08-ultra-feeding.md` and
`33-daily-nutrition-and-supplements.md` (fueling mechanics).

**Sport scope: `bike`.** **UNREVIEWED**, pending human review. The research base for 6-24 h solo
MTB is thin: mostly conference abstracts, single-athlete case reports and road data. Where no study
exists this file says so and gives `Coach judgment:` instead. Nothing here changes an engine
constant. Acute symptoms (chest pain, fainting, confusion, heat illness) override everything: stop
and use the `/coach` safety rules.

## 1. What 6-24 h MTB demands (UNREVIEWED)

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Race-level mean speeds in time-limited ultra-cycling fall steeply with
duration: about 26.2 km/h (men) at 6 h, 23.2 at 12 h and 16.4 at 24 h, per Scholz et al. (2021).
These are race means with mixed terrain and formats. `Coach judgment:` the ~29% drop from 12 h to
24 h is far larger than physiological pace decay alone; stops, sleep and night riding dominate
the 24-h number, so planning stops and night laps matters as much as watts.

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Roughly half of ultra-cycling finishers are masters riders (35-49), with a
substantial DNF rate (about 26-41%), per Abou Shoak et al. (2013). No study gives masters-specific
recovery or durability guidance for 6-24 h MTB (`Coach judgment:` treat recovery as individual).

`Coach judgment:` XC Olympic racing (about 2 h at ~90% HRmax, per Impellizzeri and Marcora (2007), a different regime) is not a template for 6 h+; do not borrow its
intensity. Illustration from the athlete's own file: a 100 km, 7.2 h XC race at NP 175 W against an
FTP near 278 W is IF about 0.63 (arithmetic on the athlete's data, not a source claim).

## 2. Pacing (UNREVIEWED)

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** At the World 24-h MTB Championships (2012-2019 editions, 837 men and 157
women, mean 275.9 km in about 23.7 h), a conference abstract by Matta et al. (2020) reports that
speed fell from the first lap in every race (positive pacing), and that first-lap speed was
inversely related to distance covered (r about -0.36), with the best-performing cluster starting
slower. Mark ⚠: conference abstract only, no lap speeds read, and the pacing conclusion is
association, not experiment. Confidence: low. Present it as "an abstract reports", never as a rule.

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** In a single elite road 24-h record, speed and power declined with distance
and the last hour showed an end-spurt (Knechtle et al. (2015); n=1, circuit, no drafting
generalization). In a short MTB race of five laps, between-lap pace was even while within-lap
speed varied widely with terrain (Martin et al. (2012); n=6, not an ultra).

**[ADAPTED: tri]** In Ironman bike legs, power, cadence and speed fell with lap number (Abbiss et
al. (2006); n=6). In time-limited running ultras, 24-h finishers had the most variable pacing
(Deusch et al. (2021)). Both fit "intensity decays over very long events" but neither is MTB.
Confidence: low-medium.
Test: compare the athlete's last-third lap times with lap 1 across a 6-12 h race; if they stay
within ~5% (the 5% threshold is `Coach judgment:`), the start was probably sustainable.

**Coach judgment:** no peer-reviewed %FTP or IF band by race duration exists for 6, 12 or 24 h MTB,
and no experiment compares even and variable pacing at 6 h+ (Abbiss and Laursen (2008) state the
ultra-endurance optimum was unknown). Everything below is Coach judgment, to be tested against the
athlete's own files, not a research result:
- Start conservatively; a fast first lap costs more than it buys (consistent with, but not proven
  by, the Matta abstract).
- Treat the athlete's 7.2 h race (IF ~0.63) as the only repo anchor; a 12 h target would sit near
  or slightly below it, and 24 h lower still, with the exact bands set from his own late-race
  decoupling.
- Use effort by feel and heart rate on climbs, power caps on the first third, and plan pit stops
  and night laps explicitly (no source quantifies stop frequency).

## 3. Heart rate declines in ultras (not a fatigue failure) (UNREVIEWED)

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** In ten elite cyclists over a 525 km road race (mean about 27 h), average
HR/HRmax fell from 0.86 early to 0.66 late, roughly 10% per 10 h, and the authors proposed an
"ultraendurance threshold" near 70% HRmax (Neumayr et al. (2004); n=10, no control group).
Confidence: medium for the direction, low for transfer to MTB.

`Coach judgment:` the analyzer and coach should expect the HR-to-power ratio to move over a 12-24 h
effort and should not call a falling HR, or a lower HR at a given power late, "fatigue failure" by
itself. The athlete's 100 km file showed the opposite sign (HR up about 7 bpm at 3% less power);
both are intensity-versus-output drift, and heat, terrain, fueling and pacing change the direction.
No peer-reviewed decoupling threshold exists for 7-24 h events. Judge drift together with lap-time
decay, RPE, fueling and symptoms, not on HR alone.

## 4. Durability and "what FTP gain should I expect from a 24-h build?" (UNREVIEWED)

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Durability is the time of onset and size of the deterioration of
physiological characteristics during prolonged exercise (Maunder et al. (2021); ~ author list
unconfirmed). The measured size depends on the protocol: more relative intensity or duration gives
larger loss (Hunter et al. (2025)). In 13 well-trained cyclists, power at VT1 fell about 6% after
150 minutes of moderate cycling, and the decline in a 5-minute TT after the same work correlated
with the VT1 durability loss (rs = 0.719), not with muscle carnosine or other tested factors
(Hamilton et al. (2024); n=13). In 14 amateurs, 20-minute power after 1,000 kJ fell about 6.5% in the
more successful group versus about 12.5% in the less successful (Barsumyan et al. (2025); n=14,
cross-sectional). Higher-level pros tolerated more prior high-intensity work before power fell
(Leo et al. (2024); short report). Confidence: medium for "durability varies and matters", low for any
number for this athlete.

**Direct answer.** Expect fresh FTP to change little from a 24-h-oriented build; the target quality
is power retained late in the ride, not a higher fresh test.
- **What the evidence says:** no controlled study of FTP change from a 24-h or ultra build was
  found. Cross-sectional work separates durability from fresh markers (it did not correlate with
  VO2max or relative FTP in one small cohort, unconfirmed authors, so `~`). The only ultra-cycling
  training data are four RAAM-team athletes over 6 months of threshold-oriented training (CP +16.8%,
  VO2max +7.1%, Manunzio et al. (2016); n=4, no control, starting status unclear), which is not
  transferable to a trained masters rider.
- **What is inference:** "little FTP change, durability is the target" is inference from the
  durability studies above, not a measured result of an ultra build. Any numeric FTP forecast is
  `Coach judgment:`; the athlete's own roughly 2-3% season-to-season rise is the only anchor the
  repo holds (`29-ftp-threshold-change-modeling.md`). A Nov-Mar block gives about 20 weeks; no
  source says how long durability takes to change.
- **How to track it (`Coach judgment:`):** repeat a fixed durability check (best 20-min power, or
  an EF comparison, after the same kJ) rather than only a fresh FTP test; see section 7.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Whether durability is trainable is untested in cyclists: no
controlled trial of back-to-back days, late-ride intervals or low-carbohydrate rides was found. The
plausible approach, and the one this file recommends as `Coach judgment:`, is quality work late in
long rides (steady tempo or threshold blocks after 3-4 h) and not dropping high-intensity work for
volume. Confidence: low.
Test: if late-ride 20-minute power retained after ~1,000 kJ improves across the build while fresh
FTP is flat, the approach is working; if retained power does not move after ~12 weeks, drop it.

## 5. Long-ride progression, recovery and taper (UNREVIEWED)

`Coach judgment:` no study gives long-ride length or back-to-back-day structures for 6-24 h MTB.
Use `31-multi-race-season-periodization.md` for how several races share a season and keep the
engine's +8% weekly and +15% long-ride rails. The athlete's bike-minute swings are route-driven,
so judge ramp by TSS where possible.

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** The one training description of an elite ultra-cycling group (Manunzio et
al. (2016)) is a threshold-weighted distribution (about 63/28/9% across three zones) for four RAAM
athletes; it is descriptive, not a prescription, and conflicts with the polarized pattern in
`24-cycling-periodization-intervals.md`.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** No peer-reviewed taper study exists for 10-24 h events; coaching
sources suggest 10-21 days, with volume cut roughly 20-30%, 40-50% and 60-70% across the final
three weeks and intensity held (not peer-reviewed). The general taper evidence in
`03-periodization.md` is the grounding. Confidence: low.
Test: a taper is sound if freshness (TSB) rises into race week without the athlete reporting
flatness; if sessions feel flat, shorten the taper.

## 6. Fueling, hydration and sleep at 12-24 h (UNREVIEWED)

Carbohydrate mechanics, gut training and EAH safety are covered in `08-ultra-feeding.md` (written for
swimming, but the carbohydrate and hyponatremia sections apply) and `33-daily-nutrition-and-supplements.md`;
this section covers only what changes at 12-24 h.

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Guidance is up to about 90 g/h of multiple-transportable carbohydrate for
efforts beyond 2.5-3 h (Jeukendrup (2014)). No study tests more than 90 g/h at 12-24 h. A 3-hour
lab study found 120 g/h raised exogenous oxidation but did not spare glycogen (Podlogar et al. (2022);
n=11, lab). `Coach judgment:` at sub-threshold ultra intensities the absolute need is lower than in
those lab studies.

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** The energy deficit is large and expected: one road 24-h rider (557 km in
22.4 h) had an HR-estimated expenditure of about 15,500 kcal against intake of about 5,600 kcal,
a deficit near 9,900 kcal, at roughly 49 g/h carbohydrate and 862 mL/h fluid (Bescós et al. (2012);
n=1, expenditure estimated from HR). Energy balance was also negative throughout a four-man RAAM
team (Lahart et al. (2013)). `Coach judgment:` intake is capped by gut and appetite, so plan to keep
the deficit sustainable, not to match expenditure; favor liquids and soft foods overnight.

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** In two 24-h MTB races, 1 of 27 finishers (3.7%) had post-race
exercise-associated hyponatremia, and faster riders drank more (Chlíbková et al. (2014); small,
Czech events). The consensus is to drink to thirst and avoid overdrinking (Hew-Butler et al. (2015)).
Optimal sodium per hour at 24 h MTB has no evidence; set it from the athlete's sweat and cramp
history (`Coach judgment:`).

**[ADAPTED: running]** Two weeks of repeated in-run carbohydrate exposure reduced GI symptoms and
malabsorption in 18 runners (Miall et al. (2018); related work by the same group is unconfirmed).
Confidence: low-medium.
Test: practice target carbohydrate on 4+ h rides; if GI symptoms and the late-ride fueling
shortfall shrink over several weeks, keep it.

**Sleep and night riding.** **[EVIDENCE: cycling]** In a four-man RAAM team, sleep deprivation
worsened progressively, riders slept under an hour per episode, and longer rest breaks preserved
sleep better; the authors recommend planning sleep, not only average speed (Lahart et al. (2013); team
relay, road). No evidence compares no-sleep with short naps in 24-h solo MTB, or gives nap lengths.
`Coach judgment:` for a 24-h solo, decide in advance whether to nap at all; caffeine timing, lights
and crew are planned from trial runs on 6-12 h rides, and any hallucination, confusion or loss of
line is a stop-and-assess signal, not a pacing issue.

## 7. Analyzing an ultra file (UNREVIEWED)

`Coach judgment:` there are no validated thresholds for these metrics at 6-24 h, so they describe
a ride and compare it with the athlete's own previous rides; none is a pass/fail.
- **Lap-time decay:** last-lap (or last-third) speed as a fraction of lap 1, the normalization used
  in the 24-h Championships abstract (Matta et al. (2020)). Report it alongside climbing per lap.
- **EF and decoupling across laps:** NP/HR by lap and the lap-to-lap trend. Interpret a decline with
  section 3: falling HR late is expected, rising HR at lower power points to heat, fueling or
  pacing. No peer-reviewed ultra-MTB validation of EF exists.
- **Durability after kJ:** best 5-20 minute power in the last third versus fresh, with the kJ done
  to that point (as in Hamilton et al. (2024) and Barsumyan et al. (2025)). Prior-work intensity
  matters as well as kJ, so also report time above tempo. The ~6.5%/12.5% loss after 1,000 kJ is a
  cross-sectional anchor from 14 riders, not a standard.
- **Stops:** moving versus elapsed time; at 24 h this, not watts, explains most speed loss.
