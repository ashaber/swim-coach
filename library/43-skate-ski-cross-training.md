# Skate-ski cross-training for a cyclist

See `00-conventions.md` for the tagging scheme and `reference_list.md` for full citations (subsection
"Skate-ski cross-training for a cyclist", grounds `43-skate-ski-cross-training.md`). Load accounting
for sessions with no power is covered in `20-cross-train-load-standardization.md` and
`15-tiered-session-load.md`; this file does not repeat them.

## 1. Scope and honesty box

**UNREVIEWED**, pending human review. **Sport scope: any athlete** (a swimmer or a cyclist may
cross-train on skis; not bike-scoped).

**No study trains cyclists on cross-country or roller skis and then measures cycling power or
time-trial performance.** Searches found no controlled study and no case series. Every claim below
about ski-to-bike transfer is therefore tagged ADAPTED (from running, swimming or
general-endurance work), or `Coach judgment:`. Treat that gap as the main finding, not a footnote.
Practitioner commentary (a physiologist Q&A, a cycling-magazine column) says transfer is plausible
but unproven and needs an adaptation period; neither cites a controlled study, so neither is used
as grounding here.

The use case: a masters MTB/cyclocross athlete replacing much of the November-March trainer base
with skate skiing ahead of a solo 24-hour race around April. The 24-hour race content itself
(pacing, fuelling, long rides) lives elsewhere and is not repeated.

## 2. What cross-training can and cannot hold

**[ADAPTED: running]** Foster et al. (1995): 30 trained runners, 8 weeks, +10% weekly training
added as running (R) or swimming (XT) against a control group (C). The 3.2-km time trial improved
in both R (-26.4 s) and XT (-13.2 s) but not C (-5.4 s); velocity at 4 mmol/L lactate rose only in
R. The authors conclude non-similar cross-training helps but less than specific training.
Confidence: low-medium (small 1995 study, running and swimming, not skiing and cycling). Test: if
bike FTP and 20-min power hold or dip slightly after a ski block while ski-specific fitness rises,
treat that as the expected specificity result, not a plan failure; a larger fall is a flag.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Millet et al. (2009): VO2max and thresholds are modality-specific
in triathletes, and transfer is likely larger running-to-cycling than the reverse. So expect a
third, ski-specific set of numbers, and expect the central engine (heart, blood volume) to carry
over better than the pedalling-specific muscle adaptations. Confidence: low-medium. Test: compare
the power-duration curve (5, 20, 60 min) before the block and at 4-week intervals during it.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Arm-trained fitness transfers poorly to untrained legs: in a
systematic review (Marterer et al., 2023; author list not fully confirmed), arm VO2peak rose in 21
of 29 interventions (+16.4%) but only 3 of 9 transfer studies found a gain in untrained-leg VO2peak
(+9.3%); the authors call transfer inconclusive. Holmberg et al. (2002, conference abstract) found
upper-body-only peak VO2 clearly below running or diagonal skiing in elite skiers. Skate skiing is
whole-body, so this is not isolated arm work; it suggests the legs carry most of the aerobic load
at higher intensity. Confidence: low. Test: if ski sessions leave HR and RPE high while the legs
feel fresh and the arms or trunk are the limiter, the session is under-training the bike engine.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Mujika & Padilla (2000): with no sport stimulus at all, trained
athletes lose VO2max, blood volume and lactate threshold within weeks, with progressive losses
after about 4 weeks that stay above untrained values. A ski block is not zero stimulus, so this is
a worst-case bound, not a prediction. Confidence: medium for the direction, low for any bike
number. Test: any fall in 20-min power greater than normal test-to-test noise at the 4-week check
means the ski block is not holding the engine.

## 3. Keeping bike fitness during the ski block

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Spiering et al. (2021): endurance performance was maintained for
up to 15 weeks with training frequency cut to as few as 2 sessions per week, or volume cut by
33-66%, provided intensity is kept; intensity is the key factor. The abstract states there are
insufficient data for athletes, so this is general-population evidence applied to a trained
cyclist. Mujika (2010) agrees that volume can fall a lot if intensity stays. Confidence: medium
for the intensity-protects-fitness principle, low for any specific bike volume. Test: track
best 20-min power and the power-duration curve every 4 weeks; if it falls more than test noise
while the bike dose was met, raise the dose.

**[ADAPTED: cycling]** Impellizzeri et al. (2005, n=12 elite cross-country MTB racers): power and
VO2 at the second ventilatory threshold, not VO2max or peak power, tracked race time. By inference
what needs protecting through winter is threshold power and muscular endurance in the riding
position, not top-end capacity. Confidence: low-medium (12 riders, correlational). Test: use
threshold-session power (sweet spot / threshold intervals) as the primary maintenance metric.

`Coach judgment:` a defensible default is 1-2 short, hard bike sessions per week all winter
(threshold or VO2max intervals, 45-75 minutes), plus an occasional longer ride for saddle time,
with skiing supplying the aerobic volume. This is a starting point built on the principle above;
the dose number itself has no source. Individualise from the 4-weekly power-curve check. Flag for
review if bike intensity sessions fall below 1 per week for more than 4 consecutive weeks.

`Coach judgment:` keep 2 heavy leg-strength sessions per week, and do not stack them on the same
day as hard ski intervals. See section 7 for the supporting concurrent-training literature.

## 4. Ski physiology versus cycling

No paper compared skate skiing with cycling at matched effort in the same athletes. **No same-athlete
ski versus bike LTHR comparison was found.** The only ski-versus-another-mode threshold work found
is ski versus running.

**[ADAPTED: running]** Rundell (1995, n=7 elite US biathlon women): ski-specific (treadmill roller-ski)
testing predicted roller-ski race results better than running tests did. The dossier's catalog record
dates this paper 1996; the index gives volume 27, which is 1995, and the library follows the index.
This study does not establish that ski threshold HR is lower or higher than run or bike threshold HR,
and must not be cited for that. Its supported lesson is specificity: test the sport you train in.
Confidence: low (7 elite women, treadmill). Test: do a field threshold test on skis (a steady 20-30
minute hard effort after a thorough warm-up); take the average HR of the last 20 minutes as ski
LTHR. If it differs from bike LTHR by more than about 5 bpm, use the ski value for ski sessions.

`Coach judgment:` until a ski field test exists, do not apply bike HR zones to ski HR. Judge early
sessions by RPE and duration. Expect heart rate to run higher than for the same effort on the bike
early in the block, because novice technique is less economical.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Millet et al. (1998, 9 skilled skiers, roller-ski V2-alternate):
self-selected cycle rate was the most economical. Skilled skiers only; a novice's economy will be
worse and noisier. Confidence: low (skilled to novice population gap). Test: if HR at a given
speed on the same route falls across the first 6-8 sessions with no fitness change, technique is
the driver, not fitness.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Elite and junior skiers spend most training time low: Seiler &
Kjerland (2006, 11 junior male skiers, 384 sessions; attribution taken from the query, abstract
only) reported about 75% of sessions in zone 1, 8% in zone 2 and 17% in zone 3 by HR (sRPE gave
76/6/18); Sandbakk et al. (2016) found world-cup women did more low-intensity and moderate-intensity
training than national-class peers. A mostly-easy-with-some-hard shape fits what the cycling files
already assume (`24-cycling-periodization-intervals.md`). Do not import elite volume. Confidence:
low (elite and junior cohorts, not masters recreational). Test: check that at least 70% of ski
time by session goal is easy and that hard days are deliberate.

## 5. Load accounting for ski sessions

Skiing gives no power, so load rests on HR or RPE. Tier logic is in `15-tiered-session-load.md`;
the no-signal case is in `20-cross-train-load-standardization.md`. Ski-specific pitfalls:

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Foster et al. (2001): sRPE tracked HR-based load across exercise
types, but absolute sRPE scores ran higher than HR-based ones, so sRPE load and TSS-like numbers
are not interchangeable without calibration. Confidence: medium. Test: over the first month of
ski sessions, compare sRPE load and HR load per session; a persistent ratio shows the calibration.

**[ADAPTED: general-endurance]** Sylta et al. (2014): in 29 elite skiers (570 sessions), HR
time-in-zone and session-goal methods gave very different distributions (about 96% versus 87%
in zone 1), with conversion factors of about 0.9 and 1.1 for zone 1 and 3.0 and 0.33 for zones 2-3.
A ski session with a few hard intervals is under-credited by pure time-in-zone HR. Confidence: low-medium
(elite skiers). Test: on interval days, compare HR time-in-zone load with sRPE load; if sRPE is
consistently much higher, time-in-zone is under-crediting.

`Coach judgment:` HR-based load needs the sport's own threshold. Applying bike LTHR to ski HR may
mis-classify zones (section 4). Until a ski LTHR exists, lean on RPE-based load and say so.

`Coach judgment:` upper-body and trunk fatigue can end a session before the heart and legs are
stressed, so HR then under-reports the cost. No quantified evidence found. Until calibrated, on ski
days take the higher of the HR-based and sRPE-based load. There is no published ski-to-bike TSS
equivalence; do not present any conversion as validated.

## 6. Ski-to-bike phasing for an April target

All of this is `Coach judgment:`; no study gives the time course of cycling power returning after a
ski block, and no source supports a number of weeks.

- **Winter block:** skiing supplies aerobic volume; the bike dose in section 3 holds threshold
  power. Re-test FTP or the 20-min power curve every 4 weeks as the data gate.
- **From about 8-10 weeks out:** reintroduce bike volume progressively while ski volume tapers.
  Reintroduction obeys the usual volume (+8% a week) and long-ride (+15%) confirmation rails; a
  large step up in bike time after a low-bike winter is a spike, not a continuation
  (`35-return-from-layoff.md` for the retraining framing).
- **Last 6-8 weeks:** 24-hour-specific long rides, saddle time and fuelling practice dominate. The
  ultra-MTB content owns the detail.
- **Gate:** if the week-8 and week-4 power-curve checks sit within test noise of the pre-block
  numbers, proceed; if they have fallen, shift weight to bike earlier. Test: the same power-curve
  comparison, so the decision is made on the athlete's own numbers.

## 7. Safety and practicalities

Keep this short; the sourced evidence is thin and mostly elite or classic-technique.

- **Falls and injuries:** Kelsall & Finch (1996, a non-peer-reviewed report): knee, arm or hand,
  and ankle were the most injured areas; risk factors included poor technique, poor tracks and
  overuse. `[ADAPTED: general-endurance]` Confidence: low (a report, mostly classic or elite
  skiers). Test: log every fall and any new joint pain; any pain report means stop and assess, per
  the standing safety rail.
- **Overuse in recreational skate skiers (shoulder, low back, hip flexors):** no evidence found.
  `Coach judgment:` ramp ski time gradually in the first weeks and watch the shoulder and low back.
- **Strength interference:** Wilson et al. (2012) meta-analysis: interference grows with endurance
  frequency and duration and depends on modality. `Rønnestad & Mujika (2014)` supports heavy
  strength alongside endurance for cycling performance `[EVIDENCE: cycling]`. Applying this to a
  ski-plus-strength week is `[ADAPTED: general-endurance]`, Confidence: low-medium. Test: if
  strength performance or key bike sessions slip on days after hard ski plus heavy lifting,
  separate them.
- **Cold air, altitude, learning curve:** no usable evidence found for recreational skiers at
  roughly 800-1500 m or for adult-beginner time-to-competence. `Coach judgment:` expect several
  sessions of elevated HR and RPE at low speed while technique improves, and use standard cold
  precautions. Any chest pain, palpitations, fainting or hypothermia signs override this file.

## 8. Open questions

1. Does any ski block preserve bike power in a trained cyclist, and how fast does it return?
2. What is a rider's ski LTHR relative to bike LTHR, in the same athlete?
3. What is the minimum bike dose when another sport supplies the volume?
4. Is there a valid ski-to-bike load equivalence, or a validated HR/RPE load model for
   recreational skate skiing?
5. How long does an adult beginner take to reach stable skate-ski economy?
6. What are the overuse injury rates for recreational skate skiers?
