## Creatine

**Dosing.** `[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: high.` A 20g/day
loading dose for 6 days, then 2g/day maintenance, raises muscle creatine
~20% (`Hultman et al. (1996)`); a plain 3-5g/day with no loading phase
reaches the same endpoint over about a month, avoiding the sharpest
water-weight gain and (by staying out of the bolus/high-dose range where
GI symptoms concentrate) most of the GI-symptom risk. **Test:** if this
athlete supplements, use straight 3-5g/day rather than a loading phase, and
note any GI symptoms against dose. The `Kreider et al. (2017)` ISSN
position stand summarizes the same dosing range and reports no compelling
evidence of harm at recommended doses in healthy adults — cite it as a
position stand, not a primary trial; several panel authors have prolific,
industry-adjacent creatine-research profiles, worth a one-line COI note
rather than treating it as fully disinterested.

**Does it help her swim?** `[EVIDENCE: swim] Confidence: high.` No.
`Huang et al. (2024)`, a 17-RCT/361-swimmer meta-analysis, found no
meaningful performance, physiological, or body-composition benefit from
creatine in swimmers — a clean null, and the most recent, most direct
evidence on this exact question. `[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence:
medium-high.` Broader endurance evidence agrees: `Fernández-Landa et al.
(2023)` found creatine ineffective for aerobic endurance performance in
trained athletes; creatine's real ergogenic case is short efforts under
~150 seconds or repeated-sprint work, not a multi-hour swim. **Test:** if
this athlete supplements, frame it to her plainly as NOT a swim-performance
intervention — track lean mass/bodyweight trend instead, the honest place
any benefit would show up.

**Strength, lean mass and bone — the strongest case for her specifically.**
`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: high.` `Chilibeck et al. (2017)`,
a 22-RCT/721-participant meta-analysis in older adults (mean age 57-70,
men and women), found creatine + resistance training added +1.37kg lean
tissue mass and greater strength gains than resistance training alone. The
direct population match — `Chilibeck et al. (2023)`, a 237-woman, 2-year
RCT in postmenopausal women doing supervised resistance training (this
specific finding rated Confidence: medium-high — a single, if large and
long, research line) — found creatine did NOT improve areal bone mineral
density at any site, but did better preserve bending-strength-relevant bone
geometry at the femoral neck, and modestly increased lean mass and walking
speed. Say this plainly: no BMD headline win, but not nothing, and no
safety signal over two years. **Test:** if she supplements, the honest framing is strength/lean-mass/
bone-adjacent (matching her 2x/week strength work per `34-kettlebell-
strength-programming.md`) and general healthy-aging, not swim performance —
and it adds body mass (water + some lean tissue), a real trade-off for a
swimmer to weigh consciously. Track lean mass/bodyweight trend and any DEXA
follow-up (`13-reds-energy-availability.md` already recommends a bone
scan as a first step) — this is a 2-year-timescale question, not one her
weekly data resolves quickly.

**The cramping/dehydration myth: directly rebutted, not just unproven.**
`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium-high.` `Dalbo et al.
(2008)` reviewed the popular claim that creatine causes cramping and
dehydration and found the controlled evidence points the OTHER way:
athletes taking creatine reported fewer cramping/dehydration episodes than
non-users, and short-term loading did not compromise hydration or
thermoregulation in dehydrated, trained men — creatine draws water
intracellularly (into muscle), a retention effect, not a deficit one.
None of this evidence is in open-water swimmers, in immersion, or in
anyone at elevated hyponatremia risk — a real population gap. **Test:**
tell her plainly that creatine does not raise cramping or dehydration risk
per the land-sport evidence available, while naming that gap.

**Creatine x EAH: an honest, untested gap — not a reassurance.**
`Coach judgment:` no study anywhere has tested creatine's interaction with
exercise-associated hyponatremia. Creatine's water-retention effect is
intracellular; EAH is a plasma-sodium-dilution problem driven by hypotonic
fluid VOLUME, per `08-ultra-feeding.md`'s existing safety rail (Hew-Butler
citations) — different compartments, so there is no obvious mechanism for
creatine to worsen EAH, but "no obvious mechanism" is this file's own
inference, not a tested finding. State it as an open question. **Test:** if
she supplements and does a long open-water swim, compare pre/post nude
body-mass and urine-output pattern (per `08`'s protocol) on a creatine day
vs. a similar-duration non-creatine day — a real-world signal on a question
the literature hasn't answered.

## Daily and periodized carbohydrate

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.` `Impey et al. (2018)`
proposes varying carbohydrate intake by the SPECIFIC session's purpose —
high availability for quality/race-pace/long sessions, deliberately lower
for some easy sessions ("train low, compete high") — rather than one flat
daily number. Her heterogeneous week (technique day, long open-water day,
race-pace intervals, strength) is exactly the shape this framework targets.
**Test:** compare next-day quality-session RPE/pace following a
deliberately lower-carbohydrate easy day vs. a flat-carbohydrate easy day,
in her own log history — exploratory, not a rule to enforce. **This is a
theoretical framework, not swim-specific outcome trials — say so.**
**Caveat that matters here specifically:** deliberately restricting
carbohydrate on easy days sits close to the low-energy-availability
territory `13-reds-energy-availability.md` already flags — present
periodization as varying intake AROUND an adequate total, never as license
to under-fuel, and cross-reference `13`'s existing caution every time this
comes up.

## Protein: per-meal distribution and pre-sleep timing

`10-recovery-hrv.md` already grounds the whole-day target
(1.65-1.83g/kg/day, `Kato et al. (2016)`) and during-exercise co-ingestion
(`Koopman et al. (2004)`) — this section adds distribution on top, not a
new daily total. `[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.`
`Moore et al. (2015)` found older men need a greater per-meal protein dose
(~0.4g/kg, ~3g leucine) than younger men (~0.24g/kg, ~2.5g leucine) to
maximally trigger post-exercise muscle protein synthesis — "anabolic
resistance." **This is men-only data; women, let alone post-menopausal
women, are not directly studied here** — the same male-skew gap `13`
already documents repeatedly for her demographic. **Test:** check whether
her per-meal protein (if tracked) clusters below ~25g on any meal adjacent
to a hard session, and flag it as worth discussing, not a rule to enforce.

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium-high.` `Res et al. (2012)`
found 40g casein protein ~30 minutes before sleep raised overnight muscle
protein synthesis ~22% after evening resistance exercise, in young men.
**Test:** on hard-evening-session days, compare next-morning wellness/
soreness on nights with vs. without a pre-sleep protein feed, in her own
log history — a low-cost addition on top of `10`'s existing post-exercise
refeed-window guidance, not a replacement for it.

## Gut-training progression rate

`08-ultra-feeding.md` already grounds WHY gut training works
(`Jeukendrup (2017)`) and THAT it works (`Miall et al. (2018)`'s 2-week GI-
symptom-reduction result) — neither pins a rate. `[ADAPTED: cycling]
Confidence: medium.` `Cox et al. (2010)` found 28 days of daily
high-carbohydrate-availability training measurably increased exogenous
carbohydrate oxidation in cyclists — a longer timescale, different outcome
measure than Miall's, corroborating trainability at a several-week horizon.
**Test:** log her tolerated in-swim carbohydrate rate against total weeks
of consistent exposure at that rate — trainability at a several-week
horizon predicts gradual, not step-change, improvement.
**No verified controlled trial specifies an exact g/h-per-week step size**
(e.g. "+10g/h every 1-2 weeks") — that figure circulates in practitioner
content (Precision Hydration, TrainingPeaks, cycling-coaching blogs) but is
not traceable to a trial. `Coach judgment:` any specific weekly step this
system adopts should ground in "progress gradually against the athlete's
own tolerance, per the trainability Cox and Miall both demonstrate," not a
claimed trial-derived number.

## Carb-loading: the female nuance (cross-refs 16, not a duplicate)

`16-race-week.md` already covers the core 36-72h, 10-12g/kg/day protocol
(`Burke et al. (2011)`, `Bussau et al. (2002)`).
`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium-high.`
`Tarnopolsky et al. (2001)` found
women, unlike men, failed to load muscle glycogen when carbohydrate rose
only as a SHARE of habitual (often lower total-calorie) energy intake — but
DID load successfully once given both a higher-carbohydrate diet and ~34%
more total energy. Practical consequence: her carb-load window needs an
absolute gram target on top of adequate total calories, not a percentage
shift alone — a failure mode ("she's eating 70% carbs now") that can look
correct on paper while under-loading her, especially given `13-reds-
energy-availability.md`'s existing concern about her habitual intake.
**Test:** N/A directly — this is a protocol-design point; the actionable
check is confirming her carb-load meal plan hits the absolute g/kg target
ON TOP OF, not instead of, adequate total calories.

## Muscle cramping (EAMC): not simply dehydration/electrolytes

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.` `Schwellnus (2009)`
argued the dehydration/electrolyte hypotheses lack a mechanism matching
EAMC's actual presentation (cramps cluster in specific, locally fatigued
muscle, not tracking whole-body fluid/electrolyte status) and that altered
neuromuscular control — fatigue-driven motor-neuron hyperexcitability — is
better supported. The more recent, more cautious `Miller et al. (2022)`
review (co-authored by Schwellnus himself) walks that back to a
confluence of individual risk factors, not one singular cause. Present
both, in order: the 2009 mechanism as the leading contender, the 2022
multifactorial view as the more current, careful position. **No swim- or
immersion-specific EAMC evidence exists at all** — immersion changes fluid
distribution and thermal context (`08-ultra-feeding.md`'s existing
Epstein 1992 citation), so whether either model transfers to a swim is
untested. **Test:** if she cramps mid-swim, treat it first as a local-
fatigue signal (ease pace/effort in that limb/stroke phase) rather than
defaulting to "she needs more electrolytes," and only weigh a fluid/
electrolyte cause alongside other EAH-consistent signs, per `08`'s existing
safety rail — never as the default assumption.

## Other supplements, briefly

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: high.` `Guest et al. (2021)`'s
ISSN position stand: caffeine at 3-6mg/kg reliably improves a range of
performance measures; individual response varies. **Test:** trial a dose
in training before race day, never for the first time on race day.

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.` `Owens, Allison & Close
(2018)`: vitamin D inadequacy is common in athletes, worse indoors/higher
latitude; unlike the general population, serum vitamin D does NOT track
bone health in athletes — but swimming's non-osteogenic nature (`13`'s own
headline finding) means she can't lean on "exercise protects bone
regardless of vitamin D" the way a land-based athlete might. **Test:**
actual deficiency status needs her own bloodwork, not inference.

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium-high.` `Sim et al.
(2019)`: iron deficiency is disproportionately common in female athletes;
exercise-induced hepcidin peaks 3-6h post-session and can suppress
absorption — timing iron-rich meals away from that window is a low-cost
lever. Menopausal status changes iron dynamics further (no menstrual iron
loss) in a way this general-population review doesn't address — a gap.
**Test:** if bloodwork ever shows low ferritin, check whether hard-day
iron-rich meals cluster right after training vs. spread out — not a
substitute for lab-guided supplementation, a medical decision.

## Gaps

No ultra-distance or open-water creatine trial exists at all — the swim
evidence stops at sprint performance (Huang 2024's null). No study has
tested creatine's interaction with EAH, in any sport. The postmenopausal
bone/lean-mass creatine literature is strong on lean mass and bone geometry
but null on areal BMD, from essentially one research group's line of
trials. The per-meal-protein/leucine-threshold literature is male-only. No
EAMC research exists in swimmers or an immersion context at all. No
controlled trial pins an exact gut-training g/h-per-week step size.
