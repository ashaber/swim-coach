## Exercise selection mapped to movement pattern

`Coach judgment:`, each citation below supports only "this movement
demonstrably does X mechanically" — a general-population/other-sport
EMG finding, not a swim-transfer or injury-prevention claim (that framing,
`[EVIDENCE: swim]`, belongs to `07`'s existing rotator-cuff citations,
untouched here).

- **Ballistic swing/snatch/clean** — `Lyons et al. (2017)` (14
  resistance-trained adults): all three drove substantial posterior-chain
  and trunk activation, differing in which muscles peak when — supports
  variety over one "best" ballistic movement.
- **Swing as hip-hinge/posterior-chain work** — `Zebis et al. (2013)` (16
  female elite field-sport athletes): the KB swing drove very high
  (73-115% MVC) medial-hamstring activation, specifically higher than
  lateral hamstring — a genuine hip-hinge/posterior-chain loading tool.
- **Turkish get-up as real rotator-cuff work, not just "stability"** —
  `St-Onge et al. (2018)` (8 subjects, bilateral shoulder EMG): the
  press-to-elbow-support and leg-sweep stages drove genuine rotator-cuff
  and scapular-stabilizer activity. This cuts both ways: real training
  stimulus, and real load on a joint that may have an injury history — the
  appropriateness question routes through `21-shoulder-health-and-load.md`'s
  return-to-sport criteria, not this EMG finding alone.
- **Carries as genuine anti-rotation trunk work** — `Ellestad et al.
  (2024)`: walking with a load produced significantly higher bilateral
  trunk activation than simply holding the same load stationary — the
  walking/gait component, not the isometric hold, drives the
  anti-rotation demand. Supports farmer's/suitcase carries as a
  defensible anti-rotation core choice, not just tradition.

## Overhead work: reframing the question, not clearing it

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.` `Hogan et al. (2021)`,
a systematic review/meta-analysis, found scapular dyskinesis alone did NOT
predict future shoulder injury across pooled athlete cohorts (one cited
923-athlete cohort: 25% injury rate with dyskinesis vs. 21% without — not
meaningfully different). The intuitive chain — "overhead work can look like
it worsens scapular mechanics, dyskinesis predicts injury, therefore avoid
overhead work" — has a broken middle link. **This does NOT clear overhead
kettlebell work for this athlete post-injury** — that is an individualized
decision through `21-shoulder-health-and-load.md`'s actual return-to-sport
criteria (pain, strength symmetry, graded exposure), not a population
statistic. **Test:** if overhead KB work is ever added, gate it on `21`'s
criteria, never on scapular kinematics alone.

**Bottoms-up press: mixed evidence, not automatically shoulder-friendly.**
`Coach judgment:` one general-population EMG source found the bottoms-up
grip increased serratus-anterior activation vs. traditional grip and a
dumbbell in male collegiate throwing athletes; a separate pilot EMG study
(10 healthy subjects) found the bottoms-up press *under*-recruited
infraspinatus relative to other kettlebell variations — a less favorable
posterior-rotator-cuff pattern than its popular reputation suggests.
Present both: bottoms-up grip is not a free pass on rotator-cuff safety
just because it "feels" more stabilizing. Any loaded overhead pressing
(bottoms-up or traditional) stays out of a template until `21`'s
return-to-sport criteria are met.

## Dosing and progression with limited bell increments

`Coach judgment:` no research exists on this, and none should be
expected to — it is an equipment-logistics question (typical kettlebell
jumps of ~4kg), not a physiology question. Standard non-load progression
levers: add reps within a range, slow the tempo, add unilateral loading,
or increase density (more work in the same time) before jumping to the
next bell size.

## Masters and postmenopausal heavy loading

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: high.` `Watson et al. (2018)`
(the LIFTMOR RCT): postmenopausal women with low-to-very-low bone mass,
randomized to 8 months of twice-weekly, ~30-minute, SUPERVISED
high-intensity progressive resistance training (deadlift, overhead press,
squat pattern) plus impact loading vs. a low-intensity home program — the
high-intensity program was safe (no vertebral fractures) and improved bone
mineral density and physical function more than the low-intensity
comparator. This directly counters the assumption that a postmenopausal
athlete should default to light loads. The transferable claim is "heavy,
twice-weekly loading is safe and effective for this demographic" — the
specific barbell exercise list is not itself a kettlebell protocol.
**Test:** if this athlete's kettlebell loading shifts heavier (a heavier
single bell, fewer reps), track any new joint pain in wellness check-ins
for the first 2-3 weeks — LIFTMOR's safety finding was under supervision,
which a home-kettlebell setup does not replicate one-to-one.

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.` `Zhou et al. (2025)`,
a 12-RCT/518-participant meta-analysis in older women with sarcopenia,
found resistance training reliably improved strength/function (grip
strength, gait speed, chair-stand performance) but had no significant
effect on skeletal muscle mass — corroborates Watson from a different
angle and sets an honest expectation: the biggest payoff in this
population is strength/function, not necessarily visible muscle-mass
change. **Test:** track function markers (a repeatable strength/mobility
check) rather than expecting a visible muscle-mass change as the marker of
progress.

## Concurrent-training scheduling, sharper than "not on pool days"

`07`'s current placement rule (strength off pool days, never the weekend)
is a stress-budget heuristic, not a tested finding. `[ADAPTED:
general-endurance] Confidence: medium.` `Wilson et al. (2012)`, the classic
21-study meta-analysis, found a small-to-moderate interference effect of
concurrent aerobic+resistance training on strength/power (not hypertrophy),
worse with high aerobic frequency/volume and same-day proximity. The
update, `Schumann et al. (2022)` (Confidence: medium-high — a more recent
systematic review that partially supersedes Wilson), found NO significant
hypertrophy/max-strength difference between concurrent and strength-only
training when volume/frequency are matched — explosive/power strength
gains may still be attenuated, especially when aerobic and strength work
happen in the SAME session. Actionable beyond `07`'s rule: same-session
pairing, more than same-day pairing per se, is the specific thing to
avoid — the maximal-strength/rotator-cuff work `07` already prescribes is
relatively protected under concurrent load; any future explosive/power
kettlebell work would be the more interference-sensitive category.
**Test:** if strength sessions stay separated from pool sessions by hours
(not stacked same-session), maximal-strength-style gains should not be
meaningfully compromised by pool volume.

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.` `Huiberts et al. (2024)`
found training status, not sex, moderates concurrent-training outcomes more
consistently across the pooled literature — a myth-check against assuming
a trained female masters athlete faces extra concurrent-training
interference by virtue of sex alone. **Test:** none needed beyond the
scheduling test above — this is a reassurance against a specific worry,
not a new rule.

## Heavy-load dosing for performance (population upgrade to `07`)

`07-strength-dryland.md`'s existing performance-transfer section cites
Rønnestad & Mujika (2014), a mixed-sex review, at `Confidence: low`.
`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.` `Vikmoen, Rønnestad et
al. (2016-2017)`, three related trials specifically in well-trained FEMALE
endurance athletes, used a reproducible heavy-load protocol shape (~4-10
reps, building toward ~85-90%+ 1RM, low weekly volume, 2x/week, added on
top of normal endurance training) and found cycling economy/performance
improved consistently, running economy less so — a closer population match
than `07`'s existing citation, corroborating the same cycling-over-running
nuance. This is a performance question, separate from `07`'s
injury-prevention framing — it does not change the engine's fixed 2x10
default. **Test:** if a strength block coincides with an improving CSS
re-test with no change in pool volume, that is a weak positive transfer
signal, same test `07` already proposes for its own citation.

## Sample session templates

`Coach judgment:` draft only, not evidence — two 45-minute sessions/week,
consistent with `07`'s `STRENGTH_SESSIONS_PER_WEEK = 2` /
`STRENGTH_SESSION_MIN = 45`, on non-pool, non-weekend days per `07`'s
placement rule.

**Session A — rotator-cuff/scapular core (unchanged from `07`) + posterior
chain:** `07`'s existing five (internal/external rotation at 90°
abduction, scapular punches, Ts, Ys), 2x10; kettlebell single-arm row,
2-3x8-10/side; kettlebell deadlift/hip hinge, 2-3x8-12; suitcase carry,
2-3x20-30m/side.

**Session B — unilateral lower body + optional Turkish get-up
progression:** kettlebell single-leg RDL or split squat, 2-3x8-10/side;
goblet squat, 2-3x8-12; Turkish get-up (partial, to elbow support, or full
depending on shoulder clearance), 3-5/side, light load; farmer's carry,
2-3x30-40m. Any loaded overhead press stays deferred pending `21`'s
return-to-sport clearance.
