# Tire pressure (CX, MTB, gravel, road: what the evidence supports, limits, how to find your own)

See `00-conventions.md` for the tagging scheme and `reference_list.md` for citations. Companions:
`45-bike-race-setup.md` (sealant, brakes, pre-race checks), `42-ultra-mtb-endurance.md`.

**Sport scope: `bike`.** **UNREVIEWED**, pending human review. Written for a masters amateur
cyclist. This file replaces an earlier version that mixed up test conditions and advice.

**Labels.** Every pressure below is exactly one of: **recommendation** (someone's advice),
**test condition** (what a study ran; never advice), **safety maximum** (a rim or tire ceiling,
never a target) or **calculator output** (a hypothesis, not evidence).

**Honest framing.** The direction is well supported: on real surfaces, above a breakpoint, higher
pressure stops being faster and may be slower. The evidence is thin and mostly commercial.
**No direct pressure-vs-speed test at CX widths (33-40 mm) was found.** No field test was found
for "25 mm at about 105 psi versus a wider tire at 60-80 psi", or for 28 versus 30 mm; do not
claim one. Too low also loses: burps, rim strikes, squirm, and in one test (below) 15 psi was as
slow as 70. **The athlete's own logged pressures on these tires, rims and courses outrank any
calculator or chart.**

## 1. Breakpoint, impedance and why drums mislead (UNREVIEWED)

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Silca (sells related tools) describes a breakpoint pressure: below it, more
pressure lowers rolling resistance; above it, surface impedance (energy lost shaking the rider and
bike) raises it again. The rougher the surface and the smaller the tire, the lower the breakpoint;
a better casing raises it (Silca (undated), calculator explainer). Heine (sells tires) puts it as
"higher pressures simply move the energy loss from tire to rider" (Heine (2024)).

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Smooth drums cannot show this. Anhalt (quoted by Zinn (2022)): below the breakpoint, road and drum curves match; smooth drums "don't properly
demonstrate breakpoint pressures"; a damped rig's damping is a small fraction of a human body's.
So drum rolling-resistance rankings show casing efficiency, and overstate the value of high
pressure on rough ground. Poertner (Silca) says the breakpoint can truly be found only with
virtual elevation field testing, and calls roll-downs "a terrible tool" for rolling resistance
(Zinn (2022)).

## 2. Virtual elevation (Chung method): what it is and its limits (UNREVIEWED)

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** The rider repeats a closed loop with a power meter and speed sensor; a
force-balance model builds a virtual elevation profile, and drag and rolling-resistance values are
adjusted until the profile closes and repeats (Chung; secondhand via Jordan (2009)). Silca (sells
related tools) advises 3 laps minimum, ideally 5-6, loops of 30 s to 5 min, varied power, low
wind, no braking, identical position (Silca (undated), aero testing guide).

**Limits, both sides.** Heine (sells tires): the method "cannot be trusted to produce reliable
results" in its current form, because testers start from guessed rolling-resistance and drag
values, and he cites 2-3% real-world precision against 0.1-1% claimed (Heine (2026); the cited
precision paper was not opened). Silca says GPS speed is often not accurate enough and gusts ruin
runs (Verhoff (2025)). One blogger's 3 W swings were not repeatable at the same pressure
(Campbell (2024); one rider, tire unnamed). A few watts is noise.

## 3. Test results that exist (UNREVIEWED)

All pressures in this section are **test conditions**, not advice.

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Soft gravel, virtual elevation: on a very soft, sand-like section described
as the roughest of the course, going 23 to 20 psi saved about 12 W, 20 to 17 about 10 W, and 14 psi
was about 2 W faster than 17. The page names no tire, run count or rider weight. The author says
not to target 14 psi (pinch flats, rim damage, only a couple of miles of the race). A soft-surface
result, not a general gravel number; commercial, undated page (Verhoff (2025)).

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Gravel lab coast-down, 700x35 with butyl tubes, riders about 150 lb:
average gap 15 psi 2.7 s, 30 psi 4.3 s (best), 50 psi 3.2 s, 70 psi 2.6 s. Too low was as bad as
70, so lower is not always faster. It could not resolve differences near 5 psi (Zinn (2018);
tubed, old).

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Roll-down, 40 and 50 mm tires on 24 mm rims, three runs per cell, rough
line: 40 mm 25 psi 1:10 versus 35 psi 1:15; 50 mm 25 psi 1:11 versus 35 psi 1:13; "across all eight
tyre width and pressure combinations, the lower tyre pressure (25psi) tested faster, on average".
Lauf-supported advertorial; a small roll-down, not proof (Cahill (2023)).

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** Rough-pavement roll-downs (charts only, seller): 38 mm at 35 psi beat 25
psi with no gain at 55; 27 mm showed no significant gain from 55 to 85 psi (Heine (2015)).

**[EVIDENCE: cycling]** MTB: a 30 mm versus 25 mm inner rim width cut rolling resistance 1.4% at
matched stiffness and raised it 0.9% at matched pressure; speed effects most likely trivial
(Maier et al. (2019); three riders, one XC course; abstract only, pressures not in it).

## 4. Road, CX and MTB: what is and is not sourced (UNREVIEWED)

**Road.** No test sets a road pressure floor. **Calculator outputs** for a 28 mm tire and a 170 lb
rider: Zipp, ENVE and Cadex about 58-61 psi, Silca 69-71 (Slowman (2023)).

**CX.** BikeRadar's 30-35 psi for 33 mm tires sits in a clincher-oriented passage, is not tied to
tubeless, and cites no data. The same article gives lower **recommendations** for tubeless and mud
(clinchers about 20 psi, tubeless 15 psi on a muddy course) (Evans (2021)). The 30-35 figure is
withdrawn as a default. A **practitioner statement about tubulars, 2018, no test data**: CX riders
on 33 mm tubulars run as low as 14 psi in super muddy conditions and about 25-30 psi in general
(Zinn (2018)). That is not a tubeless recommendation.

**MTB.** No sourced pressure recommendation was verified. Start from the athlete's own log.

**[ADAPTED: cycling]** Lower pressure than rule-of-thumb is probably faster on rough or loose ground
at CX and XC widths, bounded below by rim strikes, burps and pinch flats. Confidence: low-medium
(direction from gravel tests and one soft-surface virtual elevation result; no CX test). Test: the
protocol in section 6.

## 5. Limits and practical factors (`Coach judgment:` unless sourced)

- **Hookless safety maxima** (Schwalbe, manufacturer in-house figures): 25-29 mm 5 bar (72 psi);
  30-34 mm 4.5 bar (65 psi); 35-39 mm 4 bar (58 psi); 40-44 mm 3.5 bar (51 psi); 45-54 mm 3 bar
  (44 psi). Ceilings, not targets: obey the lower of rim and tire printed maximum.
- **Floors:** rim strikes, burping on tubeless, squirm (vague cornering) and pinch flats on tubes;
  BikeRadar warns of bottoming out the rim. Tubeless gives pinch-flat headroom at low pressure.
- **Weight:** more rider plus bike mass needs more pressure.
- **Gauges:** a six-gauge test found one read 2 psi low; the author ranked repeatability above
  accuracy (Barrett (2018)). Use one gauge and log in its numbers.
- **Temperature (computed, not a cycling study):** gas law gives about 1 psi per 10 F (5.5 C) at
  CX pressures; 25 psi set at 70 F reads about 22.8 psi at 40 F. Set at venue temperature.

## 6. Field protocol: find your own best pressure (UNREVIEWED)

**[ADAPTED: cycling]** Chung-style testing. Confidence: low-medium. Test: the protocol itself; it is
invalid if the repeated baseline pressure disagrees by more than the effect.

1. Closed loop of 1-5 min on the surface you care about, no braking, same start and finish; dedicated
   speed sensor and power meter; the same gauge every time.
2. Same bike, tire, position, clothing and bottles; weigh rider and bike; steady wind under about 3 mph.
3. Three pressures 3-5 psi apart, 5-6 laps each, order ABCBA to expose drift; repeat the middle
   pressure at the end; vary power across laps.
4. Analyse with drag and rolling-resistance starting values fixed before seeing data (answers
   Heine's criticism), in free software or a script.
5. About 90-120 min total. A few watts is within noise. Also invalid with gusts, braking, position,
   sealant or temperature changes, or no visible step across the range (widen it).
6. Keep the lowest pressure with no rim strike, burp, squirm or loss of grip. The breakpoint is
   rider- and surface-specific (Poertner).
