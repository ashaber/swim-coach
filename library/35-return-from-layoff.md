## Detraining time course: general endurance

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: high.` `Mujika & Padilla (2000)`,
already grounding this project's detraining framing elsewhere, describe a
two-phase mechanism: early VO2max loss (under 3-4 weeks) is driven mainly
by falling blood/plasma volume and stroke volume; loss from roughly 3 weeks
onward increasingly reflects falling mitochondrial oxidative-enzyme
activity. **Test:** none new — this reuses `03-periodization.md`'s existing
citation, adding only the mechanism-changes-over-time detail.

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.` `Chen et al. (2022)`
found VO2max, time-to-exhaustion, and knee-extensor strength dropped
measurably within just 2 weeks of complete cessation in young endurance
athletes — the fastest, most acute data point available, useful for
calibrating that SOME loss starts quickly even from a short interruption,
before the 5-6-month case this file addresses. **Test:** none — a
calibration data point, not a claim to falsify against this athlete's data.

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium-high.` `Burtscher et al.
(2022)`, the best masters-specific citation here, found masters athletes'
VO2max decline correlates with TRAINING-VOLUME change (explaining 54%/39%
of variance in men/women), not age itself, and that the underlying
mitochondrial/oxidative-capacity loss is "largely rescued" on a similar
timeframe of retraining. **Test:** if this athlete's return follows the
volume-ramp template below, expect fitness loss to track how much volume
actually stopped, not a fixed age-based rate — and expect it to be
substantially recoverable, not a permanent ratchet down.

## Detraining time course: swim-specific

`[EVIDENCE: swim] Confidence: medium.` `Głyk et al. (2022)` found 12 weeks
of detraining reduced lactate-threshold swimming speed and land/water
power in 14 elite swimmers (mean age ~20) — a planned transition-period
detraining, not an unplanned layoff, and not masters, but direct swim
evidence that a multi-month gap produces measurable speed/power loss.

`[EVIDENCE: swim] Confidence: medium-high.` `Tsalis & Mougios (2022)`, the
single most load-bearing swim-specific citation here, is a natural
experiment: two COVID-era training interruptions of roughly 2-4 months
each, in youth/competitive swimmers, with training load falling ~78% and
critical speed dropping ~4.7-4.9% during the interruption. **A return
period of SIMILAR LENGTH TO THE INTERRUPTION restored performance almost
fully** — only one event (men's 400m free) showed a small decline vs. a
pre-pandemic reference season. Coaches reported resuming with HIGHER
aerobic volume but NO intensity initially, before reintroducing daily
sessions with an aerobic/technique emphasis. This is the paper to lead
with for honest, reassuring framing: the return takes about as long as the
layoff, and performance comes back. Youth/competitive cohort, not masters,
and observational, not a controlled trial — say so.

## Strength is retained far longer than cardiovascular fitness

**The best-corroborated claim in this file — four independent sources
agree.** `[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.` `Encarnação et
al. (2022)`, a systematic review, found resistance-training strength gains
were maintained through roughly 16-24 weeks of detraining, becoming
statistically similar to non-exercising controls only after 32-48 weeks.
`Grgic (2022)` (Confidence: medium-high — specifically in OLDER ADULTS,
6 studies/8 groups) found no significant decrease in muscle SIZE after
12-24 weeks of cessation — a decrease appeared only after 31-52 weeks.
`Blocquiaux et al. (2020)` (Confidence: medium-high — an RCT-quality
design in 30 OLDER MEN specifically: 12 weeks training -> 12 weeks
detraining -> 12 weeks retraining, with biopsies) found only a modest -5%
to -15% strength loss over 12 full weeks off, and — the single best
citation for "regaining is faster than gaining" — retraining reached the
post-training 1RM strength level in UNDER 8 WEEKS, faster than the
original 12-week build. **Test:**
because strength itself degrades slowly, the practical first-session risk
for this athlete is not "the muscle forgot how to be strong" — see the
strength-return section below for what it actually is.

## Retraining / "muscle memory": genuinely contested, do not overclaim

This project has a specific, hard-earned reason to be careful here
(`00-conventions.md`'s account of a prior fabrication incident) — the
muscle-memory literature is a live, unresolved dispute at the mechanistic
level, presented honestly rather than picking a side.
`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.`
The foundational finding (in MICE) is that myonuclei added during training
are retained even after prolonged detraining and fibre atrophy
(`Bruusgaard et al. (2010)`) — explicitly NOT direct human evidence, only
the origin of the popular narrative. The most relevant human RCT-quality
data, `Psilander et al. (2019)` (Confidence: medium — a unilateral design:
one leg trained, one naive), found strength remained ~60% above baseline
through 20 weeks of detraining — a genuine, useful "strength outlasts
hypertrophy" finding — but found NO faster/enhanced retraining response in
the previously-trained leg vs. the naive control leg, cutting directly
against the popular "myonuclei make the comeback faster" framing. The
sharpest counter-evidence, `Rahmati, McCarthy & Malakoutinia (2022)`
(Confidence: medium-high — a large meta-analysis of 147 pooled studies),
found myonuclei were retained with atrophy in RODENTS but NOT in HUMANS —
the central mechanism behind the popular "muscle memory" story does not
appear to replicate in human data. `Snijders et al. (2020)`'s balanced
review agrees the human evidence is less consistent than the mouse data.
**Honest synthesis, do not soften into a single confident
claim:** the practical claim ("regaining tends to be faster than the
original gain, especially for strength") has real support (Psilander,
Blocquiaux above); the myonuclear MECHANISM does not, cleanly, in humans.
**Test:** if this system's coaching language ever describes "muscle
memory," it should state the practical claim, never assert the myonuclear
mechanism as settled human science.

## The core reconciliation: does a layoff return break the +8%/week rail?

**No — the rail should not be raised or bypassed. What changes is the
STARTING POINT the rail ramps from, not the rail itself.** `Coach
judgment:`, directly generalizing `22-injury-adapted-taper.md`'s already-
documented lesson for the injury-restriction case: the danger isn't the
8%/week RATE, it's silently assuming the athlete's pre-layoff peak volume
(or a stale, artificially-high recent baseline) as the starting point for
that rate. **Test:** if a return-from-layoff feature is ever built, its
acceptance test is: does the RATE of week-over-week increase ever exceed
8%? If yes, that's a bug regardless of how conservative the starting point
was — the same non-negotiable ceiling this project applies everywhere else.

**Proposed starting point, `Coach judgment:`, Confidence: low** (no source
pins this exact fraction for this population — this file's own synthesis,
not a lifted number): for a 5-6 month layoff, start the return week at
roughly 25-35% of the athlete's own last-known peak weekly swim volume — a
range chosen to sit under `Tsalis & Mougios (2022)`'s observed pattern
(return resumed at HIGHER aerobic volume than the layoff's tail, but
without intensity) while respecting that this is a masters, non-race-
runway-driven return. **Test:** if an athlete returning at this starting
fraction reports minimal soreness/fatigue relative to session RPE in weeks
1-2, that supports the fraction as appropriately conservative; persistent
excessive soreness/fatigue argues for starting lower next time.

**Arithmetic consequence, stated honestly:** climbing from ~30% back to
100% of prior peak volume purely via 8%/week compounding takes roughly
15-17 weeks. This is a feature, not a problem to engineer around — directly
consistent with `Tsalis & Mougios (2022)`'s own finding that a return took
about as long as the layoff. A "6-8 week return template" should be read
as the initial, most-conservative ramp phase (re-establishing tolerance
and technique off a near-zero base), not a claim that full prior volume
returns by week 8 — after that phase the athlete re-enters normal macro
periodization from wherever the ramp has landed, governed by the same rail
as everyone else.

## Intensity and long-swim reintroduction

`[EVIDENCE: swim] Confidence: medium-high.` Per `Tsalis & Mougios (2022)`'s
directly observed return pattern, hold intensity at low/moderate (easy
aerobic + technique) for the first 3-4 weeks regardless of how the
athlete's cardiovascular system feels — a returning athlete's subjective
effort tolerance can outrun actual connective-tissue/technical readiness.
**Test:** don't reintroduce CSS-anchored threshold/race-pace work before
week 4 of a return-from-months-off phase even if the athlete self-reports
feeling ready sooner.

**No new long-swim rule needed.** `Coach judgment:` `06-long-swim-
progression.md`'s existing `SINGLE_SESSION_STEP_CAP` and 30-day-longest-
swim lookback already generalize correctly to "returning from nothing": if
the athlete's actual logged swims in the trailing 30 days are small
(because they just restarted), the ladder's own step cap anchors off that
real, small number rather than any pre-layoff assumption — the same
insight `22-injury-adapted-taper.md`'s CTL-substitution fix already
applies (use what's actually been happening, not a stale number, and let
the existing decay/lookback mechanism do the discounting). **Test:** if a
returning athlete's early long-swim ladder ever proposes a jump not bounded
by the actual prior-30-day-longest reading, that's a bug in applying the
existing mechanism, not a reason to invent a parallel layoff-specific rule.

## Strength return: the real first-session risk

`[ADAPTED: general-endurance] Confidence: medium.` Because strength itself
degrades slowly (Blocquiaux's older-men cohort lost only -5% to -15% after
12 full weeks off), the primary first-session risk is NOT "the muscle
forgot how to be strong" — it's unaccustomed loading and reduced
connective-tissue conditioning relative to retained contractile capacity.
`O'Connor et al. (2008)` names the graded, monitored return pathway after
exertional rhabdomyolysis and explicitly distinguishes it from ordinary
DOMS (athletes often mistake early rhabdomyolysis symptoms for soreness,
delaying presentation); `Tietze & Borchers (2014)`'s clinical review names
"unaccustomed high-intensity exercise following a period of relative
inactivity" as a classic triggering pattern — precisely the mechanism of
concern for a masters athlete resuming strength work at remembered
pre-layoff loads. **Practical consequence:** do not size the first
strength session(s) back by how strong the athlete remembers being — cap
the first 1-2 sessions' volume and load conservatively regardless of
remembered capacity (a specific number is `Coach judgment:`, no citable
figure exists), avoid maximal-effort eccentric or high-volume novel
movements in session 1, and progress based on next-day soreness/recovery
response rather than a pre-set schedule. **Test:** if this athlete resumes
strength work after a 5-6 month layoff and reports soreness disproportionate
to the (modest) load used, that's consistent with the connective-tissue-lag
mechanism above and argues for holding the current load an extra session
rather than progressing on schedule.

**No swim- or strength-specific quantitative return-ramp protocol exists in
the literature — stated plainly, not papered over.** U.S. Masters
Swimming's own coach-authored guidance (Terry Heggy) deliberately avoids
giving percentage/yardage return targets, favoring individualized,
symptom-guided pacing (ease in 2-3x/week, technique-first long warm-ups,
track a repeatable weekly test set, build volume by adding a repeat or
shortening rest rather than jumping to a target distance, ramp "too flat
rather than too steep"). Generic online running-return blog figures
("30-50% of pre-break volume, then the 10% rule") are not cited as
evidence — the 10% rule itself is already documented elsewhere in this
library as not evidence-based, and repeating an unverified blog percentage
next to a debunked rule is exactly the citation-laundering this library's
conventions exist to prevent.

## Load-monitoring caveat specific to a layoff return

`Coach judgment:`, directly generalizing `22-injury-adapted-taper.md`'s
already-documented lesson: any load-ratio-based red-flag rule computed
against a 28-day chronic baseline is structurally unreliable right after a
return, because that chronic baseline is itself near-zero — even a small,
appropriately conservative return week registers as a large ratio spike,
noise rather than signal, for the same reason this project already
rejected a literal ACWR ratio for the injury-restriction ramp case.
**Test:** if a general (non-injury) layoff-return feature is ever
automated, don't gate it on the raw load-ratio threshold in the first
several weeks post-return; prefer the CTL-series-based framing this
project already built for the injury case.

## Gaps

No swim-specific quantitative return-to-volume protocol exists — U.S.
Masters Swimming's own guidance deliberately gives none. No strength-
specific quantitative starting-load percentage after a multi-month layoff
exists as a peer-reviewed source. The myonuclear "muscle memory" mechanism
is NOT established in humans, and is partly contradicted by the largest
meta-analysis available. VO2max detraining-rate figures vary widely by
population and study design — report as a directional range, never a
single precise number.
